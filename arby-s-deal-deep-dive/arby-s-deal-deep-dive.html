Private equity case study · Arby's, 2011–2026

The Arby's turnaround was real. LongRange Capital had nothing to do with it.

The premise behind this deep dive contains a mix-up worth clearing up first, because it changes who gets credit for one of the great restaurant turnarounds. Roark Capital bought Arby's in 2011 and its management team — CEO Paul Brown and brand chief Rob Lynch — rebuilt it. LongRange Capital didn't exist until late 2019. Its founder, Bob Berlin, is the thread connecting the two stories, and in 2026 he set out to run the same playbook on Pizza Hut. Here's the whole arc, with the numbers sourced and the gaps labelled.

The verdict, up front

Buyer: Roark Capital Group (Atlanta), via ARG Holding Corp. Seller: Wendy's/Arby's Group. Announced 13 June 2011, closed 5 July 2011, headline value ~$430M — of which only $130M was cash.

The turnaround: system sales $3.26B (2014) → a then-record >$3.6B (2016); average unit volume $854K (2011) → a record $1.1M (2016); Wendy's retained stake went from a $30M book value to a $450M sale in 2018. Roark has never exited — Arby's now sits inside Inspire Brands, which confidentially filed for an IPO in May 2026.

The LongRange link: founder Bob Berlin led an Arby's investment at his prior firm, The Baupost Group. What Baupost held, and how big it was, is not established in any deal document found — the 2011 SEC filing names only Roark funds. There is no LongRange purchase price for Arby's because there was no LongRange purchase.

The deal itself Per the 2011 SEC filing

Wendy's/Arby's Group had owned Arby's through Triarc, Nelson Peltz's vehicle, which bought Wendy's for ~$2.3B in 2008 and merged the two chains. The expected synergies, as Nation's Restaurant News put it in 2011, "never materialized." Wendy's announced a strategic review of Arby's in January 2011 and signed the sale on 13 June 2011; it closed on 5 July 2011, when Wendy's/Arby's Group renamed itself The Wendy's Company.

Legally, Roark's buyer (ARG Holding Corp / ARG IH Corp) bought 100% of Arby's Restaurant Group's stock, and Wendy's took part of its payment in 18.5% of the buyer's parent — which is why the press correctly shorthand it as Roark buying an 81.5% stake. What Roark got was the whole operating company and franchisor: the second-largest sandwich chain in US quick service, over 3,600 restaurants systemwide (3,437 in the US per QSR 50: 2,308 franchised, 1,129 company-owned), roughly 70% franchised. Not a franchisee, not a minority stake, not a real-estate carve-out.

The ~$430M headline value is routinely misread as $430M of cash. It wasn't:

  • ~$130M cash — paid to Wendy's/Arby's at closing (subject to adjustments). The only cash in the headline.
  • ~$30M retained stake — value ascribed to the 18.5% of ARG Holding that Wendy's kept. This stake later became the best return story in the deal (§6).
  • ~$190M debt assumed — Arby's-related obligations, primarily capital-lease and sale-leaseback debt, taken on by the buyer.
  • ~$80M tax benefit — estimated income-tax benefit to Wendy's/Arby's, expected over the following years.

Roark's own cheque, per the 8-K: its funds (Roark Capital Partners II, LP and a parallel fund) committed $180M at closing — a non-dividend preferred interest plus the 81.5% common stake — covering the $130M cash price, deal expenses and growth capital, plus up to $50M more through 2013 if needed. Press summaries round Roark's total commitment to ~$230M. Keep that number in mind: by 2015, a single dividend recap had effectively repaid it (§6).

What Roark bought: an icon with broken economics

Arby's in 2011 was the classic distressed-brand setup — high awareness, a defensible niche, and numbers going the wrong way. It was shrinking, losing money, carrying expensive leases, and presiding over a fractured franchise system after the recession. It was distressed, but not bankrupt.

Why it was underperforming

The merger hangover. The 2008 Wendy's combination distracted management and delivered none of the imagined synergies. No value platform: analysts pinned the sales slide on "a lack of value offering and intense competitive pressures in the sandwich segment" — Subway and fast-casual on one side, burger value menus on the other. Premium prices without a premium experience: tired, 50-year-old stores. A heavy company estate: ~1,129 US company-owned units (about a third of the US system) laden with capital-lease and sale-leaseback obligations. And an identity crisis: consumers filed Arby's under roast beef only, even though chicken, turkey and salads were already on the menu — "hiding in plain sight," as Rob Lynch later put it, noting that under 1% of quick-service occasions are roast beef.

The human cost of the slide

By Lynch's account, Arby's had "lost about $150,000 in sales per restaurant over a four-year period, which for a brand of our size is essentially catastrophic." Marketing was scattershot, agencies revolved, and by 2014 Arby's had the second-oldest customer base in fast food. Reported, secondary One secondary summary adds that around the sale Arby's had closed 74 franchised and 96 company-owned units and that ~350 franchisees were more than 60 days late on royalties — consistent in direction with trade-press accounts of "hundreds of locations burdened with costly leases," but not primary-verified.

The trough was already turning. Q4 2010 same-store sales rose 2% and Q1 2011 rose 5.5% — lapping deeply negative comparisons, but real momentum from a $1 Value Menu, the "Good Mood Food" campaign, and returning president Hala Moddelmog (named May 2010, her third stint at the brand). Roark bought a falling knife that had just started to level out.

"Iconic… very well known and very well liked. It owns roast beef." — Roark's Neal Aronson (via NRN, 2011), on why a franchise-focused firm wanted a shrinking sandwich chain: a distinctive position and 3,600+ units of distribution, needing capital and a playbook rather than a new concept
3

The turnaround playbook

The playbook was not financial engineering first. It ran positioning → product → marketing → remodel → franchisee trust, funded by Roark's $180M-plus and then by rising cash flow. And the decisive move came two years after closing, not at it: the management reset of 2013.

Step zero: new leadership (2013)

Moddelmog bridged the first Roark years (Angus relaunch, Market Fresh return). Then, in 2013, Roark installed Paul Brown as CEO — ex-president of brands at Hilton Worldwide, ex-Expedia. Brown hired Rob Lynch as chief marketing officer in October 2013 (ex-Taco Bell, Heinz, P&G) and George Condos as president/COO. Lynch became brand president, then Arby's president in 2017 (he left in 2019 to run Papa John's). Brown framed the stakes plainly:

"We're going to define what this brand's going to be moving forward, and it's either going to work… or Arby's isn't going to be viable." — Paul Brown, CEO, per Franchise Times' "Arby's do-or-die moment"

The positioning: "Fast Crafted"

Brown's team refused the QSR-versus-fast-casual binary. In guest sorting exercises, people consistently placed Arby's "right in the middle, straddling the line between fast food and fast casual" — so the brand claimed the middle on purpose: fast crafted, food quality above traditional quick service, speed and convenience above fast casual. Every other move below is that sentence executed.

Product: protein-first, premium + value ladder

The organising principle was Lynch's: "the way people think about food… almost always starts with the protein." Arby's would be the place for great meat sandwiches — all meats, not just roast beef.

Signature run, 2013–16: Smokehouse Brisket (Oct 2013 — brisket smoked 13 hours by a small Texas family supplier, sliced fresh in-store); Mega Meat Stacks (2014); gyros; loaded curly fries; Sliders (31 Aug 2015 — mini sandwiches across proteins); the first pork-belly sandwich in quick service (2016); a venison sandwich that sold out in all 17 hunting-market restaurants (2016); 24 culinary innovations in 2016 alone.

Pricing architecture: instead of a margin-crushing dollar menu, Sliders became the value platform — "value focused, not just on price, but on a wide variety of high quality proteins" (Brown) — at a low ticket, while premium limited-time items lifted average check. Sliders sold 29 million in September 2015 alone and over 160 million by end-Q3 2016.

Marketing: "We Have The Meats"

The inherited campaign, "Slicing Up Freshness," had "failed to move the needle," and franchisees wanted the CMO responsible gone. Lynch ordered an agency review on arrival; Fallon won it (~Jan 2014) and launched "We Have The Meats" — Ving Rhames' baritone, meats slamming onto a cutting board, aimed squarely at younger guests to fix the ageing customer base.

Earned media as strategy: a glass-walled social-listening room (the "Fish Bowl," Dec 2013); the Grammy-night tweet at Pharrell Williams' Arby's-like hat (~80,000–85,000 retweets; Arby's later bought the hat when Williams auctioned it for charity); and embracing Jon Stewart's running Daily Show mockery — lunch deliveries after every jab, a job link tweeted at his retirement, farewell ads in Aug 2015, Brown and Lynch in the finale's front row. Lynch called them "big, calculated risks" that rivals outspending Arby's roughly 10-to-1 wouldn't take.

Restaurants: the "Inspire" remodel

The new design — tested in 2013 across 14 units in Greensboro, NC and Erie, PA — was deli-inspired: wood tones, warm tile, communal tables, Wi-Fi, white countertops, a cooler case displaying meats before slicing, wraparound awning, upgraded lighting and landscaping. Leaner formats followed (urban inline, end-cap drive-thru, a "Delight Line" production layout cutting square footage).

Cost discipline: after iteration, remodel cost was cut ~$75,000 to the low $300,000s per restaurant. Payoff: remodelled company units averaged ~20% sales increases in the early cohort (QSR, 2014); the company's 2015 release put remodelled restaurants at 15% or more, some over 20% — a cohort difference, cited here as a range. Rollout: 179 remodels in 2015, 229 in 2016.

People: "Brand Camp" put every company-restaurant employee through a half-day off-site session on the brand and its "make it right every time" promise — 19,000+ team members by Sep 2014.

Franchisees: trust first, refranchising later

The turnaround-era move was the opposite of the classic PE playbook: Arby's kept its large company estate as the remodel and product test lab, and Brown ran an open, "nothing off limits" consultation reset that the biggest franchisees (US Beef Corp's Jeff Davis; MarLu's Tony Lutfi) credit as the key change after Roark's early "growing pains."

Restored confidence showed up in commitments: US Beef agreed in Jan 2016 to add 70 locations over seven years; Arby's signed development deals for 167 new restaurants in 2016 — the first new-restaurant growth in a decade. (The mass refranchising came much later, in 2025, and belongs to a different era — §6.)

Supporting levers: the Efficiency Matters programme saved >$20M via energy behaviour and HVAC/oven/lighting upgrades. Digital in this era was social- and loyalty-led (receipt-scan campaigns across 3,400+ stores), not app-first — the app-scale digital story only arrives with Inspire (§6).

The results: a genuine operating turnaround

The numbers through 2016–17 are not a financial-engineering mirage: same-store sales, system sales and unit volumes all inflected, and an outside party — Wendy's, marking its retained stake to market — corroborated the value creation. Two cautions ride along: most operating figures are company-reported, not audited (Arby's is private; no audited P&L is public), and the streaks prove a unit-economics turnaround, not durable unit growth (§8).

Unit economics

Average unit volume: $854K (US, 2011) → $1.1M through Q3 2016, a record — up more than 25% in four years, and +40% since the 2010 trough (NRN, Feb 2017, citing Wendy's investor presentation).

Margins: from ~5% EBITDA (2011 analyst estimate) toward the "normal" low-to-mid-teens range for the category. Not audited No Arby's EBITDA series is public; the recovery is inferred from dividend capacity, S&P's 2015 note of "improving operating margins thanks to store closures and efficiency efforts," and the valuation path below. Any precise post-turnaround margin figure should be treated with caution.

Outperformance: 2015's +8.0% was more than 3× a sample of larger quick-service chains; 2016's +3.8% beat the category by 2.3 points, with 11 consecutive quarters of transaction growth. Market share: one 2025 secondary summary put Arby's at roughly a third of the US sandwich market in 2024, behind Subway and Panera — Single source, unverified; no primary QSR/Technomic series was found, so it isn't charted here.

6

Exit & current status: there hasn't been one

Roark has not exited Arby's. The brand today is one of six inside Inspire Brands, Inc. (Atlanta) — Arby's, Buffalo Wild Wings, Sonic, Jimmy John's, Dunkin', Baskin-Robbins — majority-backed by Roark, with more than 33,300 locations and >$33.4B in sales per May 2026 IPO coverage. What exists instead of an exit is (a) one clean minority exit by Wendy's, and (b) dividend recaps that returned Roark's money early. Both are worth understanding precisely, because together they're the only quantified return evidence available.

The one clean exit: Wendy's, not Roark

Wendy's 18.5% stake was diluted to 12.3% when it declined to fund its pro-rata share of the Buffalo Wild Wings acquisition. On 16 August 2018 it sold that stake back to Inspire for $450M (~$335M after tax; a 38% premium to its prior carrying valuation). Total take on a stake received for free as deal consideration: $30M initial book value → >$100M in distributions over seven years → $450M sale, roughly $550M+ gross. Restaurant Business called the $30M → $450M move a "1,400% increase over seven years" — note that's growth on book value, not a cash-on-cash return, since Wendy's invested no new cash in the stake.

Roark's interim returns: the dividend recaps

In 2013, Arby's parent borrowed $370M to fund a dividend. In 2015, it completed a $790M dividend recapitalisation — refinancing debt and paying out roughly $300M of dividends, of which Wendy's received $54.9M and Roark and other investors received >$240M. NRN's arithmetic: against Roark's ~$230M total commitment, "with that one dividend, [Roark] repaid its investment" — two years before the value peaked. S&P simultaneously moved Arby's credit outlook to negative on the debt load, while acknowledging sales momentum and improving margins. Undisclosed Roark's fund-level IRR, multiple, or post-recap basis on Arby's/Inspire has never been published, and Baupost's proceeds — if any — are unknown. Any source quoting a Roark IRR for Arby's is inventing it.

From repaired brand to platform: Inspire Brands

The endgame of the turnaround was not a sale — it was a roll-up. Arby's repaired cash flow and credibility funded the $2.9B Buffalo Wild Wings acquisition (closed 5 Feb 2018; $157/share, ~38% premium; BWW brought 1,250+ units and ~$3.9B of system sales, plus Rusty Taco), and the combined company launched as Inspire Brands with >4,600 restaurants and >$7.6B in system sales. Then came Sonic ($2.3B, Dec 2018), Jimmy John's (2019 — one later analysis reports $830.1M plus a $313.4M tax-receivable agreement; contemporaneous releases didn't disclose a priceReported) and Dunkin'/Baskin-Robbins (Dec 2020). Brown's stated logic: "a different kind of restaurant company" using scale "not only to save cost, but also to enable outsized investments in long-term growth" — shared supply chain, data and loyalty platform, development capability — with an ambition of no more than ~10 brands of $1B–$4.5B in system sales each.

What remains of the turnaround today

The brand assets endure: "We Have The Meats" is still the slogan, the Fast Crafted positioning and Inspire design language persist, and Brown remained Inspire's CEO through the period covered by sources. Operationally, though, Arby's is no longer Inspire's growth engine — US units shrank in each of 2023, 2024 and 2025; in 2025 franchisees opened 136 restaurants but closures, non-renewals and cessations outweighed them, and the 115-unit refranchising cut the company estate to 921. In the IPO coverage, the growth story is led by Dunkin' (Inspire's largest brand, ~$15.5B system sales) and BWW GO, not Arby's. The possible next chapter is the Inspire IPO: confidentially filed in May 2026, proceeds earmarked to repay term-loan debt — reported and pending, not a completed exit for Roark.

The LongRange epilogue: Pizza Hut, 2026

On 16 June 2026, Yum! Brands agreed to sell Pizza Hut in two deals totalling $2.7B: LongRange Capital pays ~$1.5B for the non-China business (plus up to $75M of earn-out to 2030) and Yum China ~$1.2B for China. Both deals have now closed — Yum China completed the China purchase on 7 August 2026, and LongRange completed the ex-China acquisition on 1 September 2026. Coverage framed it explicitly as a replay — Inc.'s headline: "He Saved Arby's. Now He's Betting $1.5 Billion That He Can Rescue Pizza Hut." That framing is exactly how the conflation this page opened with keeps spreading: Berlin's Baupost-era Arby's work is real, but Pizza Hut is LongRange's first restaurant deal, and it doesn't retroactively make the firm Arby's owner. The man behind that headline gets his own section next (§7).

Bob Berlin: the man in the middle of both stories

Every version of the Arby's–LongRange confusion runs through one person. Robert "Bob" Berlin is founder and managing partner of LongRange Capital — and, a decade earlier, he was the Baupost investor whose private-equity portfolio included Arby's. Headlines compress that into "he saved Arby's." The record supports something narrower and, on inspection, more interesting: Berlin is an operator-turned-investor who specialises in complicated, flexible deals — carve-outs, turnarounds and recapitalisations — and who has now bet his own firm's reputation on doing the restaurant version again, for real this time, with Pizza Hut.

The path: operator first, investor second

Berlin did not start in finance. Per his LongRange bio, he was an executive at two technology firms, including CFO and head of strategic planning at DSL.net, a broadband company he brought public. That operating chapter matters because it is the credential he still leads with: LongRange describes its approach as drawing on "direct operating experience," and Berlin's deal statements almost always frame him as a partner to incumbent management rather than a replacement for it.

From technology he moved to J.H. Whitney & Co. as a partner, sourcing and leading carve-outs, turnaround equity and credit-restructuring investments across consumer, software, healthcare and business services. In other words, the specialisation — businesses in transition, separated from a parent, or in need of a reset — was set before Baupost, and it has never really changed since. He holds a B.S. with Honors from Washington & Lee University. Beyond that, the public record on Berlin personally is thin: no profile found gives his age, family, or personal fortune, and this page does not guess at any of them. Not in sources

Baupost, 2008–2018: the decade that made the reputation

At Boston's Baupost Group — Seth Klarman's value-investing firm, not a conventional buyout shop — Berlin was, in his bio's words, "principally responsible for private equity investments" from 2008 to 2018. He led a team making flexible investments in private companies — majority, minority and joint-venture structures, carve-outs, turnarounds and recapitalisations — across consumer, value-added industrials, technology and financial services. The named investments aggregate to more than $5 billion in total enterprise value: Arby's, Archroma, Bakkavor, Central Pacific Bank, Innovia and PDC Brands, among others.

His method, as a board member of public and private companies, is described the same way in deal after deal: help management drive value through innovation, accretive capital investment, new commercial relationships, organisational upgrades and operational optimisation. Note what is absent from that list: cost-cutting programmes and financial engineering. Whether that self-description matches outcomes is harder to test — Baupost does not publish deal-level returns, and neither does Berlin — but it is consistent with how he has structured LongRange, below.

The Bakkavor thread shows the pattern in miniature. Bakkavor, the UK fresh-prepared-food maker, was a Baupost investment Berlin knew from inside the boardroom: he served as a non-executive director from 2016 to 2018. Six years after leaving Baupost, he bought the relationship back — see the LongRange record below.

What Berlin actually did — and didn't do — at Arby's

This is the claim the whole page turns on, so it deserves precision. Three things are established: (1) Berlin's own bio lists Arby's among the Baupost private-equity investments he was principally responsible for. (2) 2026 coverage repeats it in stronger language — AP said Berlin "previously engineered Arby's turnaround leading PE at Baupost," and Inc. headlined him as the man who "saved Arby's." (3) The 2011 deal documents name only Roark funds as sponsor and equity funders; Baupost's instrument, stake size, entry price and exit proceeds appear in no source found (§9).

What that adds up to: Berlin was an investor in the Arby's story, plausibly a significant one, at a firm whose exact position is undocumented in public sources. He was not the sponsor (that was Roark), not the operator (the turnaround was executed by Paul Brown, Rob Lynch and their team from 2013 — two years into Roark's ownership), and not acting for LongRange (which would not exist for another eight years). Crediting him with "engineering" the turnaround elides the difference between backing a deal and running a brand. The fair statement is the one his bio makes: Arby's was one of the investments he was responsible for, inside a >$5B portfolio — and it is the example the market now reaches for every time his name comes up.

"He Saved Arby's. Now He's Betting $1.5 Billion That He Can Rescue Pizza Hut." — Inc., 16 June 2026, on the Pizza Hut announcement. The headline is the conflation in its purest form — and also, as marketing for Berlin's next deal, extremely effective

2019: a false start, then his own firm

Berlin left Baupost in 2018 and in early 2019 became the first investment partner to join Navab Capital Partners, the firm being built by former KKR executive Alex Navab. Navab died unexpectedly in July 2019; the still-forming firm shut down, and its team scattered to Blackstone, TPG and elsewhere. Berlin's plans were publicly unknown as late as November 2019; per his LinkedIn profile as reported by Institutional Investor, he officially launched LongRange in December 2019.

The launch came with an unusual structure that drew scrutiny. LongRange's first fund closed in September 2020 with over $1.5B in commitments, and CalPERS — the California public pension giant — was the sole investor, a person familiar with the matter confirmed to Institutional Investor; the firm's owners added $15M of their own money alongside. The arrangement prompted governance questions at CalPERS, where some board members argued a commitment of that shape resembled the system's "Pillar IV" long-term investing strategy, which required specific board approval; CalPERS board president Henry Jones responded that the investment team "has acted within their delegated authority and has fully briefed the board." Both the criticism and the response are reported here as they were reported then — the episode is part of Berlin's record, and so is the fact that a single pension entrusted a first-time firm, essentially built around one man's Baupost track record, with a billion and a half dollars. Reported

The firm's stated model explains the name: control and minority investments of $50M–$400M in middle-market businesses with revenues over $200M, in non-discretionary consumer, food and beverage, information and data services, packaging, industrials, specialty chemicals and specialty distribution — taking, in its own words, "a longer-term perspective to investing" through a "company-focused and customer-first philosophy," rather than a conventional three-to-five-year flip.

LongRange backed Greencore's offer for Bakkavor (announced May 2025; combination completed January 2026), took cash plus shares, became one of Greencore's largest shareholders, then reduced its holding in February and July 2026 and exited the remainder in full (announced 28 July 2026).
US Synthetic Closed 21 Jul 2025 Another carve-out: the Orem, Utah maker of polycrystalline-diamond (PCD) technology — 600+ patents, serving energy, mining and industrial markets — bought from ChampionX for $300M (per counsel Paul, Weiss), closing only after SLB completed its ChampionX acquisition. President Rob Galloway, in the role since 2009, stayed on as CEO. Berlin praised its "exceptional culture of continuous improvement" and "focus on innovation and customer service."
24 Hour Fitness Announced 7 Jan 2026 Berlin led LongRange's acquisition of the company-owned gym chain alongside its founder, Mark Mastrov, who returned as owner and executive chair — the same founder-restoration move as backing incumbent teams, one step further. Existing CEO Karl Sanft stayed; the business employs ~7,000 people and hosts 113M+ workouts a year. Berlin: "24 Hour Fitness has an iconic legacy and significant opportunity ahead." The chain had been through a 2020 Chapter 11 that shed $1.2B of debt — a turnaround situation, again.
Pizza Hut (ex-China) Closed 1 Sep 2026 The largest and most public test of the Berlin thesis — detailed below.

Read together, the portfolio is the Baupost job description executed under his own name: carve-outs from parents that no longer wanted the asset (Hillenbrand, ChampionX, Yum!), a minority position in a business he already knew from the boardroom (Bakkavor), a post-bankruptcy icon with its founder restored (24 Hour Fitness) — and in every case, the existing chief executive kept and publicly partnered with, not replaced.

Pizza Hut: the Arby's script, run in the open

Pizza Hut is where the Berlin story stops being biography and becomes a live experiment. The parallels with Arby's in 2011 are the ones Berlin himself is selling: an iconic, heavily franchised brand (founded 1958; ~$10B in system-wide sales; the chain behind the Original Pan and Stuffed Crust pizzas), shrinking inside a parent focused elsewhere (Yum! keeps KFC, Taco Bell and Habit Burger), with sales, footprint and market-share erosion over a decade-plus and roughly 250 US closures expected in 2026 alone. Yum! sold it in two pieces totalling $2.7B — China to Yum China ($1.2B, closed 7 Aug 2026) and everything else to LongRange.

LongRange completed the ex-China acquisition on 1 September 2026 for approximately $1.5B, with Yum! eligible for a further $75M earn-out by 2030 tied to Pizza Hut's performance. Leadership changed at the close: global CEO Aaron Powell (in the role since 2021) announced on 14 August that he was stepping down — LongRange offered him a continuing role and he declined — and Eduardo Luz, CEO and president of P.F. Chang's from 2023 to 2025, with earlier senior roles at Panera Bread and Kraft Heinz, is leading Pizza Hut on an interim basis. Global chief development officer Joseph Call also departed at closing.

"Pizza Hut has a storied legacy and global footprint that represents a significant opportunity. We are committed to supporting our franchisees and making the right investments to help Pizza Hut deliver consistently great food and experiences for guests around the world, while executing the operational and digital initiatives that will drive sustainable growth." — Bob Berlin, on completing the acquisition, 1 September 2026

Strip the press-release language down and the promised playbook is recognisably the Arby's sequence: franchisee support first, capital for the brand, operational and digital upgrades, patience from a private owner sheltered — as Restaurant Dive put it — from "the immediate pressures of public markets." The sceptics are on the record too: BTIG analyst Peter Saleh noted in June that "the valuation is a bit higher than we expected and going private could enable a more drastic turnaround effort, but the track record of similar deals isn't great," and analysts cited the loss of pizza delivery's old structural advantage to third-party aggregators as a headwind no owner change removes.

What would prove the Arby's parallel — and what is still unknown. At Arby's, the signals that the turnaround was real arrived in a specific order: a management reset, a sharpened positioning, product news, remodel economics, then franchisee development commitments. For Pizza Hut, only the first step has happened (interim leadership, ownership change). There is as yet no announced positioning reset, menu overhaul, remodel programme or development data under LongRange in the sources found, and no disclosed LongRange return figures on any deal — Batesville, Bakkavor/Greencore or otherwise — against which to grade Berlin's "longer-term" model. The Bakkavor exit (in January 2024, fully out by July 2026, via a combination he supported) is the closest thing to a completed LongRange chapter, and its price and return were not disclosed either. Berlin's reputation was built inside other people's firms, on terms the public never saw; Pizza Hut is the first time the bet, the buyer and the man are all the same name on the press release. It is far too early to call it a repeat of Arby's — and now, at least, the attribution will be accurate.

Lessons: why it worked — and what it didn't fix

Why it worked

  1. Buy the brand, fix the positioning. Roark paid a distressed price (~$130M cash; ~5% EBITDA margins) for durable distinctiveness — "owns roast beef" — and 3,600+ units of distribution. The value creation was overwhelmingly operational (AUV +25–40%, the sales streak), not multiple arbitrage.
  2. Patience and capital, then a management reset. Roark funded $180M + $50M and waited two years before installing the Brown/Lynch team that actually executed. Early product and value steps stopped the bleeding; the 2013–16 system compounded it.
  3. Narrow the identity, widen the menu. "Fast Crafted" plus protein-first innovation solved the core paradox — famous for a protein in a <1%-of-occasions category — without abandoning the hero: premium limited-time items for perception and check, Sliders for value and trial, roast beef for identity.
  4. Marketing as participation, not interruption. "We Have The Meats," the Fish Bowl and embracing mockery (Pharrell, Stewart) generated earned reach a sub-scale advertiser outspent ~10-to-1 couldn't buy — and franchisee pride followed the buzz, unlocking remodel and development commitments.
  5. Franchisee trust as a leading indicator. Brown's consultative reset preceded the development deals (167 in 2016; US Beef +70). The 2016 return of unit growth — the first since 2008 — is best read as a franchisee-confidence metric.
  6. Use a repaired brand as a platform. Arby's cash flow and credibility funded Buffalo Wild Wings and Inspire — the classic Roark roll-up: scale in supply chain, data/loyalty and development, then buy bigger brands (Sonic, Dunkin').

What didn't work / the limits

  1. Unit growth was the weakest leg. Even at the 2016 peak, net growth was modest (60 openings systemwide), and by 2023–25 the US estate shrank again (−150 units over three years). The turnaround fixed unit economics far more durably than unit count.
  2. Dividend recaps pulled cash out early and levered the company. The 2013 ($370M borrowing) and 2015 ($790M) recaps returned Roark's capital quickly but drew an S&P negative outlook. That's return acceleration, not deleveraging — and it complicates any claim that all the value came from operations.
  3. The turnaround didn't inoculate the brand. Post-2018, Arby's momentum faded relative to its Inspire siblings; by 2024–25 it was closing more US units than it opened. On public evidence, the 2014–16 playbook hasn't solved the post-COVID value, labour-cost and trade-down squeeze.
  4. Attribution is genuinely messy. The public story conflates four actors (Roark the sponsor, Wendy's the retained minority, Baupost the unquantified co-investor, Berlin the individual) and two eras (Roark 2011–18 vs. LongRange 2019–). Any case study crediting "LongRange Capital" with the turnaround — or quoting a LongRange IRR — is repeating that conflation.
  5. Key numbers are company-claimed, not audited. Sales streaks, remodel lifts (+15–20%) and AUV come from franchisor releases, sometimes via NPD samples. They're directionally corroborated by Wendy's stake valuation and dividend capacity, but no audited Arby's P&L is public.

Open questions — what could not be verified

These gaps are stated plainly rather than filled. If a number you were expecting isn't on this page, it's probably here.

  • Baupost's exact Arby's position (2011). Size, instrument (equity co-invest, debt, an LP interest in Roark funds, a later secondary purchase), entry/exit price and return are all unknown. The 2011 8-K names only Roark funds; later bios assert an investment without terms. Unresolved.
  • Roark's fund-level return. No IRR, multiple or post-recap basis has been disclosed for Arby's or Inspire. Only Wendy's proxy ($30M → $320M → $450M + >$100M distributions) and the recap arithmetic are public.
  • Arby's standalone financials. No EBITDA/margin series (2011–present) and no standalone valuation today, inside private Inspire.
  • Dunkin' Brands price conflict. $8.8B (WSJ's 2026 IPO note) vs. $11.3B (multiple other sources) — most plausibly equity value vs. enterprise value including assumed debt, but no source found reconciles the two. Both are reported here; neither is picked.
  • Jimmy John's transfer price (2019). The $830.1M + $313.4M tax-receivable figures appear in one later analysis only; contemporaneous releases said the price was not disclosed.
  • Pre-deal distress specifics. The ~350 late-paying franchisees, 74 + 96 unit closures and a "$35M loss at sale" figure come from a secondary summary, and the loss figure conflicts with the dated $50.7M 2010 operating loss. This page uses the dated figure.
  • Market share. The "~one-third of the US sandwich market (2024)" claim is single-source with no Technomic/QSR primary table behind it.
  • Inspire IPO outcome. The May 2026 confidential filing and the reported ~$20B valuation / ~$2B raise are uncompleted and unconfirmed; no listing date or ticker in sources found.
  • Pizza Hut close — resolved. An earlier edition of this page listed the close as unconfirmed. It is now confirmed: LongRange completed the ~$1.5B ex-China acquisition on 1 September 2026 (§7). What remains open is everything after the close — no turnaround results under LongRange exist yet.
  • Berlin / LongRange returns. No IRR, multiple, or deal-level return has been disclosed for Berlin's Baupost investments or for any LongRange deal, including the completed Bakkavor/Greencore exit (price undisclosed).
