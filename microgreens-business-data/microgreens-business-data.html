1 · Market size and growth

The global microgreens market is on the order of $3.0–3.5 billion in 2025, growing at roughly 11–12% CAGR toward $8+ billion by ~2033. The direction is consistent across firms; the absolute values are not — estimates for the same year range from $1.02B to $4.10B depending on whether sprouts, baby greens, seed kits, and cosmetics are counted, and whether value is measured at farm gate or retail (retail runs 2–3× farm gate at $25–50/lb). market-research estimate

Reliability B− — strong agreement on growth direction; absolute values are methodology-dependent, and Grand View Research specifically has a documented inflation bias. Don't budget against any firm's TAM; use local demand evidence instead.

Living trays vs. cut greens

Living-tray sales — restaurants cutting to order, or DTC at $10 per 10×10 tray — avoid harvest labor, packaging, and shelf-life risk entirely. Cut greens in clamshells command the $3–6/oz retail band but add packaging cost (~$0.30–0.80/tray) and waste exposure. industry article

Are prices falling? Unknown — grade C

Evidence is thin and mixed. Onthegrow (2026) states "pricing has generally increased due to rising input costs." SPIN-Farming-era grower anecdotes (~2014) show $2/oz retail versus $2–4/oz today — meaning nominal retail prices have held roughly flat to slightly up over a decade, so real (inflation-adjusted) prices have declined. Saturation and competition are flagged specifically "in some urban areas." Treat price compression as a reported risk, not a measured trend — no longitudinal wholesale price series was found.

Seed cost — the unit that matters is per tray, not per pound

  • True Leaf Market radish (Red Rat's Tail): 1 lb $19.18 · 5 lb $60.71 ($12.14/lb) · 25 lb $231.14 ($9.25/lb). At ~1 oz seed per tray: $0.76–1.20/tray. vendor prices read from the store's Shopify product feed, Oct 2026; per-tray figure author's calc
  • Bulk bands: basic brassicas (radish, broccoli) $8–15/lb; pea/sunflower $10–18/lb; organic certification adds $3–7/lb; 5–10 lb bags cut unit price 15–25%. True Leaf Market overall $9–16/lb vs. Johnny's Selected Seeds $12–19/lb. industry article
  • Sunflower seed runs $2–4/lb at ~2 oz/tray — very low cost per tray. Peas need heavy seeding (~8 oz/tray), so at $10–18/lb seed they cost $5–9/tray — cheap per pound of output, expensive per tray. industry article + author's calc
  • Basil seed $30–60/lb at a small seeding rate. Specialty outliers exist — marigold seed up to $350/lb (Todd's Seeds). Variety selection drives seed cost more than anything else.

Electricity — Oklahoma's edge

A 45W LED panel at 16 hrs/day uses 0.72 kWh/day industry article. Oklahoma's average residential rate was 13.13¢/kWh in 2025 and 14.35¢/kWh in July 2026 (EIA data via priceofelectricity.com) — among the 10 lowest states. So one 4-ft shelf (2× 20W Barrina-type bars ≈ 40W) at 14¢/kWh, 16 hrs/day costs ≈ $0.09/day ≈ $2.70/month; a 10-shelf operation ≈ $25–30/month, consistent with cultivationag's estimate. Per tray, electricity is $0.15–0.30 — small. Fans and dehumidification add on top. author's calc

Growing media

  • Loose coco coir brick $8–15 (expands to ~10L) ≈ $0.90/tray; pre-cut coco pads $1.50–3.00/tray. industry article
  • Hemp mats (Terrafibre) $38.99 per 10-pack of 10×20 = $3.90/tray — the premium, compostable option. vendor (westcoastseeds.com)
  • Soil/potting mix is cheapest but heavier and messier for living-tray sales.
Labor and waste decide the margin

Labor: 100 trays/week ≈ 10–15 hours for an experienced grower; at $20/hr that's $200–300/week of implicit labor (cultivationag). A single 24-tray rack is manageable by one person "but only with a system" (onthegrow).

How small rack growers actually compete

The shared failure mode of the big farms (verticalfarmdaily quoting IGS/McWin): vertical farms replace free inputs — sunlight, rain — with expensive ones — electricity, engineering. It only pencils out for crops worth enough per kg, and most food is not. Microgreens are one of the few crops that clear the bar.

  • Living trays cut to order — a freshness no national shipper can match. As microgreensworld puts it: "80 Acres Farms supplying 17,000 Walmart locations does not compete with a grower supplying 20 Atlanta restaurants with same-day delivery." industry article
  • Specialty varieties with no local competition (shiso, popcorn shoots, sorrel) = pricing leverage.
  • Hyperlocal story, direct chef relationships, and no corporate buyer approval gauntlet.
  • Entry costs ~$300, so the moat is route density, reliability, and relationships — not technology.
  • Saturation risk is real in dense urban markets, but less so in mid-size metros where supply still lags chef demand (cultivationag).

6 · Oklahoma: rules, competitors, buyers

The legal picture comes from microgreensguru's Oklahoma analysis, which cites Oklahoma statutes (63 O.S. §1-1118), the ODAFF grower guide Bringing Farm to Market, and tax code directly. It's a secondary legal explainer, not the agencies themselves — confirm with ODAFF before acting on it.

Reliability A− for the legal analysis (statute + agency-guide citations, via a secondary source) · B for competitor and market facts (growers' own sites and newsletters)

The rules that matter

  • No license needed for your own raw microgreens. 63 O.S. §1-1118(A)(1) exempts a produce stand offering "only whole, uncut and unprocessed" produce. ODAFF's guide explicitly treats microgreens grown in soil/substrate and harvested above the soil line as produce, not sprouts — covering both harvested leaves and live trays. The harvest cut does not end the exemption in Oklahoma (other states reach the opposite answer on similar wording).
  • Live trays must travel and sell in a tray, not a bag, so water/soil don't contact leaves (ODAFF).
  • Not sprouts: because roots stay behind in the medium, microgreens sit outside the federal sprout rule — but worker-hygiene and water controls can still apply above the federal coverage floor of $34,324 three-year-average produce sales (2023–25). ODAFF pages still show the old $25,000 figure.
  • Cottage food law is the wrong tool. The Homemade Food Freedom Act ($75,000 cap) — becoming the Local Food Freedom Act at $250,000 effective November 1, 2026 (HB 3720, signed May 5, 2026) — does not apply to raw produce at all.
  • Processing changes everything: washing/packaging beyond raw produce, salads, or prepared foods triggers OSDH food-establishment rules — plan review $425, application, pre-licensure inspection.
  • Insurance: no Oklahoma law requires product liability for an owner-only raw grower, but product eaten raw makes it strongly advisable — and grocery/restaurant contracts set the real requirements (certificates, additional-insured, lot traceability). No universal state dollar limit is published.
  • Zoning is the real gate: Oklahoma City limits home occupations (no on-premises shop/store sales as of right) — check city/county planning before producing in a residence. Business entity: Secretary of State registration $100 + fee; LLC annual filing $25.
  • Contacts: OSDH Consumer Health 405-426-8250 · ODAFF Food Safety 405-522-6119 (also the number for free ODAFF on-farm readiness reviews).
The tax trap

Oklahoma's Agricultural Exemption Permit (68 O.S. 1358.1) exempts farm input purchases — but OAC 710:65 lists commercial greenhouses among activities that do NOT qualify as farming. An indoor rack grower should get a written Tax Commission determination before assuming seeds, trays, lights, and media are exempt. Separately: direct-to-consumer produce sales are exempt from state sales tax (effective Aug 29, 2024; local taxes may still apply), and a sales/use tax permit costs $20 + handling.

Customer acquisition — what growers report working

  • Free sample drop-offs to chefs (pea shoots + sunflower + radish), a one-page spec sheet (crops, flavor notes, shelf life, pack sizes, prices), follow up in 3 days. Reliability beats variety — on-time weekly delivery wins accounts. anecdote
  • "Chef boxes" and free samples of new varieties with a feedback loop — microgreensfarmer runs a 4-question Google Form after 30 days, then yearly.

Two honest gaps

Churn: no published churn or retention data for microgreens restaurant accounts exists. Growers consistently say accounts are lost on missed deliveries and quality slips, and kept via consistency, feedback loops, and seasonal variety rotations — directional and anecdotal, not measured.

Grocery consignment: no Oklahoma-specific consignment terms or fee schedules were located. Buyers commonly require a supplier agreement, certificate of product and general liability insurance, a UPC and price file, case and lot traceability, delivery temperature expectations, product specifications, payment terms, and a recall procedure — get the buyer's vendor manual before pricing.

9 · Risks and failure modes

Ranked roughly by how often growers report them and how fast they kill margin.

Reliability B — grower consensus plus one peer-reviewed shelf-life paper; insurance figures are industry rules of thumb
01

Mold and damping-off — the #1 reported crop killer

Pythium and surface molds thrive on overwatering, poor airflow, and domes left on too long. Prevention consensus: bottom-water (dump standing water), fans on every rack, room humidity <60% (ideal 40–50%), fresh medium each cycle, sanitized trays (H₂O₂ or vinegar), domes off at germination. If mold covers >25% of a tray or stems collapse — compost the whole tray; do not sell or "clean" it. Seed pre-soaks in dilute food-grade H₂O₂ are debated (some growers report harm to beneficial microbes); process hygiene matters more than any spray. Most vulnerable: sunflower, peas, amaranth, some brassicas. (onthegrow mold guide; Bootstrap Farmer; farmstandapp)

02

Shelf-life logistics

Cut microgreens are highly perishable. study Berba & Uchanski (Journal of Young Investigators): 14 days (arugula, red cabbage) to 21 days (radish) at 4°C; only 7 days at 10°C. Mustard microgreens (MDPI Foods 2023): 14 days at 5°C, 1 day at 20–25°C. Store dry and unwashed; wash immediately before use. The cold chain (harvest → cooler → delivery) is a hard requirement for cut greens — living trays sidestep it entirely.

03

Price compression and saturation

Near-zero entry barriers (~$300) mean any restaurant corridor can sprout competitors; documented in some urban markets. Defense: same-day freshness, living trays, specialty varieties, route density (§2, §5).

04

The labor scaling trap

At 100 trays/wk you're at 10–15 hrs of experienced labor. The jump to 150–300 trays/cycle demands irrigation automation, 20–30 hrs/wk, and usually hired help — this is where margins compress and burnout hits. (fernandshelf; cultivationag)

05

Restaurant demand seasonality

Summer patio/heat patterns and holiday closures create order dips. Diversified channels (markets, subscriptions) smooth revenue. anecdote consensus

06

Food-safety liability

Eaten raw, no kill step. No Oklahoma insurance mandate for owner-only raw growers, but buyers will require certificates. Industry rules of thumb: general liability from ~$500/yr; product liability $700–1,000/yr for $1–2M limits (farmstandapp; urbanaglaw.org); ~$1,000/yr per $1M coverage (Produce News quoting Western Growers). Workers' comp applies once you hire in Oklahoma.

07

The processing-line trap (Oklahoma-specific)

The day you wash and seal retail packs beyond raw-produce handling, you acquire a license, a $425 plan review, and inspections you didn't have before. (§6)

08

Failure is usually financial, not horticultural

The vertical-farm graveyard (§5) is the macro version — Plenty, Bowery, AeroFarms, and AppHarvest all failed on unit economics and capex. At small scale, Tulsa's Abundant Microgreens stepped back from farmers markets in 2024 after 7 years while keeping wholesale and delivery — a real local example of channel retrenchment.

What "making a living" actually requires

Side income starts at ~10 trays/wk (a few hundred dollars a month). A full-time living needs roughly 100–300 trays/wk (100–300 lb/wk) across 10–20+ restaurant accounts plus markets and subscriptions — $3,000–8,000+/month revenue at 40–60% net → $1,500–4,000+/month net, at 20–30 hrs/wk of labor at 150–300 trays/cycle. Labor by scale: 10 trays/wk ≈ a few hrs/wk (30 min/day checks + 2–4 hrs seeding/packing + 1–2 hrs delivery); 100 trays/wk ≈ 10–15 hrs; commercial scale ≈ 20–30 hrs/wk; one full-time grower (Nordic Hydro) reported 8–12 hrs/day at large scale. industry article anecdote

Open questions — what the research could not verify

  • USDA-funded sources were excluded per the brief. The University of Missouri Extension microgreens enterprise budget (g693, 2024 — USDA Farm Service Agency funded) and the SARE grant final report FNE20-966 (SARE is USDA NIFA-funded) contained the most detailed small-scale enterprise budgets found. Their headline numbers (18–32% net margins per tray; $2,326 startup recovered in year one) circulate in secondary articles — several of which (speedcalcs, uaorganic, homesteadingalliance) build directly on the Missouri budget — but they were not used as evidence anywhere on this page.
  • No longitudinal wholesale price series exists publicly — "price compression" is a reported risk, not a measured trend.
  • No published churn/retention data for restaurant microgreens accounts, and no Oklahoma grocery consignment fee schedules were found.
  • No audited small-grower P&Ls — profitability benchmarks are anecdotes and industry estimates.
  • Live price verification failed — the research browser was unavailable, so vendor prices (Bootstrap Farmer tray 10-packs, Johnny's seed SKUs) are index-sourced. Confirm before purchasing. Local HVAC/dehumidification quotes for an Oklahoma grow room are an open item.
  • Legal/tax: the Oklahoma analysis is a secondary legal explainer — confirm licensing, the agricultural-exemption determination, and zoning with ODAFF (405-522-6119), the Tax Commission, and city/county planning before investing.
