Part 1 — Landfill gas capture: how it works

Landfill gas capture is one of the oldest and most proven climate technologies we have — and it is having a second boom because of Renewable Natural Gas (RNG).

Gas production

When organic waste — food, paper, yard waste — decomposes without oxygen deep inside a landfill, it creates landfill gas. It is about 50% methane, 50% CO2, plus trace compounds. Methane is 80x more potent than CO2 over 20 years, so letting it vent is a major problem.

Collection

Modern landfills are engineered with a Gas Collection and Control System (GCCS). That is a network of vertical wells and/or horizontal trenches drilled into the waste, connected to header pipes, with a blower/vacuum pulling the gas out. EPA requires this system for landfills designed to hold 2.5M+ metric tons.

What happens next — 3 paths

1. Flare it

Simplest. Burn methane to convert it to less-potent CO2. No energy value, just compliance.

2. Generate electricity on-site

Clean the gas slightly, then burn it in internal combustion engines, turbines, or microturbines to make power. That is the classic LFGTE — Landfill Gas to Energy — model.

3. Upgrade to Renewable Natural Gas

This is where the money is now. Clean and separate the gas to ~95%+ methane, so it is pipeline-quality. It can then be injected into the natural gas grid, used as vehicle fuel (CNG/RNG for trucks and buses), or as boiler fuel for nearby industry.

A typical large landfill can produce gas for 20–30 years after closure, making it a long-lived asset.

How the gas is used

  • Electricity: A 3 MW landfill project powers about 2,000 homes continuously.
  • RNG injection: The gas becomes indistinguishable from fossil natural gas and qualifies for RIN credits under the EPA Renewable Fuel Standard (RFS) and Low Carbon Fuel Standard (LCFS) credits in California — that is what makes the economics work.
  • Direct use: Nearby factories, greenhouses, or wastewater plants can burn the gas directly for heat/steam, replacing fossil fuel.
  • Vehicle fuel: Compressed RNG for garbage trucks — Waste Management and Republic Services run part of their own fleets on it.

Who does this as a business

The industry has three types of players:

1. Big waste owners who monetize their own landfills

Waste Management (WM), Republic Services, Waste Connections, Biffa, SUEZ/Veolia — they own the landfills and operate or lease out the gas systems.

2. Specialized developers / operators — they build and run the plants

Ameresco, Aria Energy (now part of BP's Archaea Energy), EDL Energy, DTE Energy Biomass, Granger Waste Services, LMS Energy, BioCNG — these companies are the core builders of LFGTE systems. Ameresco and Veolia are consistently listed as top global players in the LFGTE market.

3. Pipeline and energy off-takers

Kinder Morgan, NextEra Energy Resources, BP — they buy, transport, or market the RNG. Kinder Morgan has multiple RNG interconnection projects. BP acquired Archaea Energy for $4.1B in 2022 specifically to scale landfill RNG.

Equipment providers: INNIO (Jenbacher engines), Kohler, GENERON, Clarke Energy make the engines, membranes, and upgrading skids.

Newer entrants are focused on monitoring and optimization — e.g., Envirosuite launched an emissions monitoring module in 2025 to improve methane capture rates.

Economics to know

Landfill gas projects make money 4 ways:

  1. Electricity sales
  2. RNG sales
  3. Environmental credits (RINs + LCFS — can be worth more than the gas itself)
  4. Avoided methane penalties / compliance

The catch: capture is never 100%. EPA assumes 60–90% collection efficiency depending on cover type and well density. Wells need constant tuning, and older unlined landfills often leak.

Oklahoma context: Oklahoma has ~8 active LFGTE projects per EPA LMOP. The closest large examples are in the OKC metro and Tulsa, most operated by WM Renewable Energy or EDL.

1. East Oak Landfill — Oklahoma City — WM

This is the big reference plant for the state. WM opened its RNG facility there in 2022. It takes the raw landfill gas, runs it through Morrow Energy cryogenic equipment to strip CO2 and nitrogen, and injects pipeline-quality RNG into the commercial gas network. WM says it will heat 14,000+ homes or fuel about 500 heavy-duty trucks.

It was also the site of an early gas-to-liquids pilot — making diesel and wax from landfill gas next to the landfill.

~45 miles from Norman · Watch this one

2. Southern Plains Landfill — Grady County

This is the one to watch on the business side. Waste Connections owns the landfill, Vision RNG is the developer/operator.

Vision RNG announced it as LFG-to-RNG with an option to build two more at Waste Connections' Red Carpet and Osage Landfills, also in Oklahoma. First plant projected at >370,000 MMBtu of RNG per year, avoiding 16,464 tons of CO2 annually. Construction jobs + full-time operators.

Vision RNG is backed by Vision Ridge Partners — their model is: they finance, build, own, operate; the landfill gets a royalty; they sell RNG + RINs.

3. SE OKC Landfill — Oklahoma City — Republic Services

Republic has an RNG facility design using membrane-based CO2 removal — ESI provided the full facility design for that project. Part of a 4-project deal Republic did with energy partners in Canton MI, Millington TN, Memphis TN, and OKC.

Slightly farther, but important context

Scissortail Renewables — Enid City Landfill (~90 miles N) — Broke ground Jan 2024. Sparq Renewables (OKC-based) + City of Enid + NextEra Energy Resources. First municipally-owned landfill in Oklahoma to produce pipeline-quality RNG. Gas goes into the ONEOK pipeline to heat homes. This is the template cities are copying.

American Environmental Landfill — Tulsa — Montauk Renewables broke ground April 2025, ~1,500 MMBtu/day variable inlet design.

Why everyone in Oklahoma is switching from electricity to RNG

Old model: Burn gas on-site, make 2–5 MW electricity, sell to OG&E at ~$25–40/MWh. Thin margins.

New RNG model: Collect the same gas and upgrade it to pipeline-quality RNG instead — capturing RNG sales plus RIN / LCFS credit value on top of the gas itself (see Economics, above).
