Biochar and Activated Carbon for Water Cleaning and Contaminant Removal

An evidence review of materials, mechanisms, performance, applications, risks and research gaps

Prepared October 22, 2026 · Numbered citations link to the reference list at the end

Key findings at a glance
  • Both materials are carbon-rich solids made by heating biomass (or, for activated carbon, also coal) with little oxygen. Activated carbon goes through an extra activation step that greatly expands its internal surface area [5][6][7].
  • Activated carbon is the established, regulated workhorse. The U.S. EPA lists granular activated carbon (GAC) as a Best Available Technology for PFAS, and it is widely used for organic chemicals, taste, odor and chlorine [6][13].
  • Pound for pound, biochar usually adsorbs less than activated carbon. It is much cheaper and has a far smaller carbon footprint, and one review found it removes about 3.7× more pollutant per euro spent [11][24]. Performance varies hugely with feedstock and production conditions [8][23].
  • Neither material disinfects water. Carbon filters do not reliably remove bacteria or viruses, so microbiologically unsafe water still needs disinfection or a suitable membrane [6][21].

1. What the materials are and how they are made

Biochar

Biochar is the solid, carbon-rich product left when biomass is heated with little or no oxygen (pyrolysis). Feedstocks include woodchips, crop straws, nut shells, bagasse, manures and other solid wastes [2]. Pyrolysis temperature, heating rate, residence time and feedstock type control its properties [1][3]. Higher temperatures generally give more surface area, porosity, pH and mineral content but fewer oxygen-containing functional groups [9]. Typical biochar has a low apparent density (0.30–0.45 g/cm³) and a surface area of about 200–400 m²/g, with micro-, meso- and macropores [4]. Feedstock chemistry matters too. In one comparison at the same temperature, cellulose-derived biochar had a larger micropore area (280 m²/g) than lignin-derived biochar (200 m²/g) [2].

Activated carbon (activated charcoal)

Activated carbon (AC) covers a broad family of carbonized materials with high porosity and surface area [5]. Drinking-water GAC is made from bituminous coal, lignite, peat, wood, coconut shells and other porous raw materials. Physical and/or chemical processing creates and enlarges its pores [7].

  • Physical activation: the char is treated with an oxidizing gas such as steam or CO2.
  • Chemical activation: the precursor is impregnated with an agent such as KOH, H3PO4, ZnCl2, K2CO3 or NaOH. A review of 81 studies found phosphoric acid commonly used for lignocellulosic materials at lower temperatures. Zinc chloride gave more surface area than phosphoric acid but is used less because of environmental concerns, and KOH outperformed NaOH on surface area and efficiency [5].

The result is an extremely porous material. A University of Georgia Extension bulletin notes that AC may have about 1,000 m² of surface area per gram [6]. That is several times the 200–400 m²/g typical of raw biochar [4]. In short, biochar is pyrolyzed but usually not activated. Activated carbon is a char that has been deliberately activated to maximize pore development, and biochar itself can be used as the starting material for activated carbon [5].

2. How they remove contaminants

Which removal mechanisms dominate depends on the contaminant and on the sorbent's surface chemistry. The main ones are:

Production temperature sets the dominant mechanisms. Biochar made at low pyrolysis temperatures keeps oxygen functional groups and suits inorganic and polar contaminants (electrostatics, precipitation, ion exchange). High-temperature biochar has more surface area, microporosity and hydrophobicity and suits organic contaminants [1][3].

3. Performance by contaminant class

3.1 Heavy metals (Pb, Cd, As, Cr, Hg)

Different mechanisms dominate for different metals [9]:

  • As: complexation and electrostatic interactions.
  • Cr: electrostatic interactions, reduction and complexation.
  • Cd and Pb: complexation, cation exchange and precipitation.
  • Hg: complexation and reduction.

A synthesis of 559 Langmuir isotherms from 133 studies, covering 17 metals, found the highest sorption values for Pb(II), Cr and Cd(II) [8]. Biochar sorbed Pb(II) and Cd(II) better than As(V) and Zn(II). Feedstock was the key determinant: biochars from nutrient-dense feedstocks (animal biowaste, sludge, manure) outperformed woody-plant biochars, and chemical modification was the most effective post-treatment [8].

Anionic arsenic is the weak point of unmodified biochar. One pinewood biochar (600 °C) had a maximum As(V) capacity of only 0.3 mg/g at pH 7 [2]. Activated carbon, for its part, removes "a few metals like mercury" and metals bound to organic molecules but not most metal ions [6]. Even so, the CDC notes that many carbon pitcher filters are marketed to remove lead [21], so certification labels should be checked for the specific metal. A life-cycle meta-analysis concluded that, per unit mass, biochar's heavy-metal capacity can compete with or exceed activated carbon for the metals it analyzed [24].

3.2 Organic chemicals, pesticides and pharmaceuticals

Activated carbon is described as the best method available for removing organic chemicals from household water. It also removes VOCs, some pesticides, gasoline components, trihalomethanes and benzene [6]. EPA notes that GAC also removes natural organic matter, synthetic organic compounds and disinfection-byproduct precursors [7].

For organics, biochar generally adsorbs somewhat less than AC. In a boxplot meta-review, mean maximum equilibrium capacities were about 95,000 mg/kg for biochars versus 176,800 mg/kg for activated carbons [11]. The gap was largest for more hydrophobic compounds. For medium non-polar compounds, average biochar efficiencies fell in the same range as AC, which suggests biochar could replace AC in some tertiary treatment [11]. For antibiotics such as sulfonamides and tetracyclines, the main pathways are π–π electron donor–acceptor interactions, hydrogen bonding and electrostatic/hydrophobic effects [10].

In field-oriented work in Thailand, high-temperature gasifier biochars removed the herbicide 2,4-D below detection (2 µg/L, i.e., ~100%) from water starting at 100 µg/L with background organic matter. Traditional kiln charcoals ranged from essentially no uptake to ~80% removal [23].

3.3 PFAS ("forever chemicals")

EPA identified GAC, anion exchange, reverse osmosis and nanofiltration as Best Available Technologies for its 2024 PFAS drinking-water standards [13]. GAC works well on longer-chain PFAS such as PFOA and PFOS. Shorter-chain PFAS such as PFBS and PFBA adsorb less well [12]. Powdered activated carbon (PAC) is less efficient and economical than GAC for PFAS [12].

Biochar's record on PFAS is mixed:

  • GAC ahead: in a direct comparison, GAC's maximum Langmuir capacities for PFOS (123.5 µmol/g) and PFOA (86.2 µmol/g) were 43% and 39.6% greater than a softwood biochar's. GAC also reached equilibrium faster (3–24 h vs 12–48 h) [14].
  • Commercial biochar ineffective: in rapid small-scale column tests on municipal wastewater effluent, a commercial biochar was ineffective for PFOA, PFOS, PFHxA and PFHxS [17].
  • Engineered biochar can compete: a reed-straw biochar made at 900 °C removed >92% of short-chain PFAS at ~1 µg/L. At 100 µg/L, GAC removed 87% of PFOA but only 13% of PFBA [15]. Brief air oxidation of biochar at 400 °C raised PFAS solid–water distribution ratios by up to three orders of magnitude, in some cases to GAC-comparable levels [16].

Figure 1. Short-chain PFAS slip through GAC far more readily than long-chain PFAS. A purpose-made, high-temperature biochar performed well on short chains in one study [15]. A commercial biochar was ineffective in a separate wastewater test [17].

3.4 Nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus)

A Water Research review found that unmodified biochars adsorb ammonium (NH4-N) poorly, generally below 20 mg/g, mainly via ion exchange and oxygen functional groups [18]. Nitrate affinity is even lower because negatively charged biochar surfaces repel it. Phosphate is removed mainly by precipitation with metals and metal oxides. Metal-modified biochars remove NH4-N, NO3-N and PO4-P significantly better. Biochar can also release its own N and P into water [18]. Activated carbon does not remove nitrate [6].

3.5 Pathogens

Carbon is not a disinfectant.

  • Activated carbon: AC does not remove bacteria [6]. The CDC states that most pitcher and fridge filters, which typically use carbon, are not designed to remove germs [21]. Tightly compressed solid-block carbon can physically strain out Giardia and Cryptosporidium cysts [6]. Carbon filters can also become breeding grounds for bacteria if poorly maintained [6].
  • Biochar in stormwater: amendment can improve retention of fecal indicators. A review of bioretention studies reported E. coli removal ranging from about 30% to 98% depending on biochar type and modification [19]. A 61-week simulated-field study found that biochar's E. coli advantage over sand disappeared after about 31 weeks, likely because of biofilm growth. After conditioning, biochar filters out-performed sand by only ~0.3 log units [20].

3.6 Color, taste, odor and chlorine

This is AC's signature strength. Activated carbon removes unpleasant tastes, odors (e.g., trace hydrogen sulfide) and residual chlorine [6], and GAC can improve water's color, taste and smell [13]. Chlorine is destroyed by catalytic reduction to chloride within seconds in the first few inches of a new bed. Chloramine reacts more slowly [6]. Utilities dose PAC against the algal earthy/musty compounds MIB and geosmin, which conventional treatment does not remove. Higher doses are needed when turbidity rises above ~26 NTU [22].

4. Applications

Point-of-use (POU) and household filters

Pour-through, faucet-mount, under-sink and whole-house (point-of-entry) carbon devices are common [6]. Small faucet units hold too little carbon for adequate contact time and are not recommended for organic-chemical removal [6]. The CDC advises checking for NSF certification: Standard 42 covers taste and odor, and Standard 53 covers cyst reduction [21].

Municipal GAC and PAC

Utilities pass water through GAC contactors or filter beds, usually near the end of the treatment train [13]. When contaminant "breakthrough" reaches an unacceptable level, the spent carbon is replaced with virgin or reactivated GAC. Reactivation is typically high-temperature thermal treatment in a multiple-hearth furnace or rotary kiln [7]. PAC is dosed directly into water and removed with the clarification sludge, which is useful for seasonal taste and odor events [12][22].

Stormwater biofilters

Biochar is increasingly used as an amendment in bioretention systems and rain gardens. It improves retention of microbes and some nutrients, but performance declines with aging and biofilm formation [19][20].

Wastewater polishing

GAC can polish municipal effluent for PFAS but is costly. One study estimated US$900–1,400 per million gallons (30-year amortized) for 50% PFOA/PFOS removal, roughly a 60% increase over the estimated current wastewater-handling costs for the City of Toronto [17]. The authors suggested upstream source control may be more feasible [17]. Biochar has been proposed as a cheaper tertiary sorbent for moderately non-polar organics [11].

Low-cost household and community filters in developing regions

Aqueous Solutions has deployed multi-barrier systems in Thailand and on the Thai–Burma border that use gravel, biologically active sand and locally made gasifier char [23]:

  • A portable 300 L/day system that costs about US$125 to build. It meets minimum drinking needs for ~100 people.
  • A 3,000 L/day farm system that costs under US$500. Its char is refurbished every two to three years.

Gasifier stoves reach the 650–950 °C needed for well-developed porosity [23]. The biosand stage, not the char, provides the biological barrier in these designs.

6. Modified and engineered biochar

Pristine biochar has limited selectivity, so researchers modify it to enrich surface sites, pore structure and functional groups [3]. Documented strategies include:

  • Mineral, metal-oxide and nanoparticle loading: loading with minerals, reductants, organic functional groups and nanoparticles, plus alkali activation, to raise metal sorption [9]. Metal-modified biochars capture far more NH4-N, NO3-N and PO4-P because surface charge shifts and metal oxides increase [18].
  • Chemical post-treatment: the best post-pyrolysis option in the 133-study metals synthesis. Chemically modified, washed or magnetized biochars showed higher metal affinity [8].
  • Thermal oxidation: brief 400 °C air oxidation enlarged pores and boosted PFAS sorption up to 1,000-fold. Spent biochar could then be thermally reactivated at 500 °C, with even higher PFAS affinity in a second cycle [16].
  • High-temperature, long-residence pyrolysis: 900 °C for 5 h produced the reed-straw biochar that removed short-chain PFAS [15]. Longer pyrolysis was also linked to better metal sorption [8].
  • Activation: chemically activating biochar effectively turns it into activated carbon, which narrows the performance gap but adds cost [2][5].

7. Limitations and risks

No pathogen kill. Neither biochar nor AC disinfects water. Activated carbon does not remove bacteria [6]. The CDC recommends ultrafiltration, nanofiltration or reverse osmosis for bacteria, and nanofiltration or reverse osmosis for viruses [21]. Carbon filters can harbor bacterial growth [6], and removing chlorine can let germs grow in plumbing [21].
  • Saturation and breakthrough: sorbents fill up. Once effluent exceeds a target concentration, the carbon is "spent" and must be replaced or reactivated [7]. A saturated GAC filter can release organics back into treated water [6], and other "stickier" contaminants such as organic carbon can displace PFAS [13]. Household cartridges must be changed on schedule; a six-month interval is common [6].
  • Competition and water chemistry: background organic matter lowered short-chain PFAS removal by biochar above ~8 mg/L dissolved organic carbon [15]. Turbidity raises the PAC dose needed for taste and odor control [22]. pH and co-existing ions strongly affect sorption [3].
  • Leaching from the sorbent:
    • GAC can release certain metals (particularly arsenic, antimony and iron) and cause pH swings and loss of disinfectant residual [13].
    • Biochar can release its own nitrogen and phosphorus [18].
    • PAHs formed during pyrolysis are a concern for biochar, but they are bound so strongly that freely dissolved PAH concentrations measured by equilibrium sampling were in the pg–ng/L range, generally near or below environmental background [25].
    • Biochars made from sludges or contaminated feedstocks warrant testing before drinking-water use.
  • Variability: biochar quality differs widely by feedstock and process. Traditional kiln charcoals ranged from no herbicide uptake to ~80% removal [23], and metal sorption results were highly variable across studies [8].
  • Lab-to-field gap: biochar's E. coli advantage in stormwater filters faded after ~7 months of field-like conditioning [20]. Most studies test single contaminants in clean water, unlike real mixtures [3].
  • Regeneration and disposal: spent carbon holds whatever it captured. Some filters (e.g., those used for radon) may need hazardous-waste handling [6]. PAC used for PFAS produces PFAS-laden sludge [12]. GAC reactivation needs specialized high-temperature facilities [7]. Composting and land application of spent filter char has been suggested for pesticide-laden char in rural settings [23], but it would be inappropriate for persistent contaminants such as PFAS or metals.
  • Removing beneficial substances: filters can strip chlorine (a disinfectant) and fluoride [21].

8. Practical takeaways

  1. Test first, then choose. Identify the specific contaminants, and pick a device certified for them (NSF 42 for taste/odor, NSF 53 for health-related claims such as cysts) [21].
  2. For taste, odor, chlorine and most organic chemicals, activated carbon is the proven choice [6]. Use enough carbon and contact time. Larger under-sink or point-of-entry units outperform small faucet units [6].
  3. For PFAS, use treatment validated for PFAS: GAC, anion exchange or RO for utilities [13]. Expect short-chain PFAS to break through GAC earlier [12]. Do not assume a generic biochar will work [17].
  4. Always pair carbon with disinfection or a suitable membrane if the source may carry pathogens [21].
  5. Replace media on schedule and flush units that sit unused, because spent or idle carbon can release contaminants or grow bacteria [6].
  6. Biochar fits best where cost, local production or carbon footprint dominate: metal-laden industrial or mine water [8], stormwater biofilters [19], and off-grid multi-barrier systems for pesticides [23]. Make it at high temperature (e.g., gasifiers at 650–950 °C) and from consistent feedstock [23].
  7. Match the biochar to the target. Use high-temperature char for organics, low-temperature or mineral-rich (manure, sludge) char for cationic metals, and metal-modified char for phosphate or anions [1][8][18].

9. Research gaps

  • Field and column validation: most biochar data are from batch tests. Column tests at realistic scale and long-term field studies are needed [4][11][20].
  • Multi-contaminant, real-water performance: competitive sorption among co-existing metals and mixed pollutants is poorly understood [3][9].
  • Standardized characterization: the lack of consistent physicochemical and performance characterization prevents robust links between biochar properties and removal capacity [11].
  • Regeneration, reuse and end-of-life: biochar reuse and cost reduction are open questions [9]. Safe disposal of spent sorbents loaded with persistent contaminants (PFAS, metals) needs study [16].
  • Short-chain PFAS: promising engineered biochars need scale-up and validation in natural waters with organic matter [15][16].
  • Nutrient leaching and selectivity: endogenous N/P release and competing ions limit nutrient applications [18].
  • Aging and biofilm effects on biochar filters, and in-situ regeneration strategies [20].
  • Decentralized quality control: simple field methods to verify that locally made char is adequately sorptive [23].
