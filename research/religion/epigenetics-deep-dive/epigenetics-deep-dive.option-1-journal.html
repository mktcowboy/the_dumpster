Epigenetics, from Waddington’s landscape to the clinic

Your genome is the hardware; the epigenome is the layer of chemical annotations that decides which parts of it get read, in which cells, and for how long — without changing a single letter of DNA. This page walks the whole field: the 1942 concept, the molecules that write and erase the marks, the mouse and famine cohorts that proved environment can move them, the clocks that read them as age, the drugs and CRISPR editors that now rewrite them — and the inheritance claims that still don’t hold up. Every key claim is tied to the paper that made it.

The working definition

Epigenetics is the study of heritable changes in gene expression or chromatin state that are not attributable to changes in DNA sequence — marks stable enough to survive cell division, written onto DNA and its packaging proteins.

Operational definition after Berger, Kouzarides, Shiekhattar & Shilatifard, Genes & Development 2009.

Section 01

Origins: a word in search of a mechanism

Epigenetics began as a developmental-biology idea — how does one fertilized egg, with one genome, produce a neuron and a liver cell that breed true through division? — decades before anyone knew the chemistry. The word came first; the molecules took another half-century.

1942

Conrad Waddington coins the “epigenotype” in a short Endeavour essay — the causal network of gene interactions that produces a phenotype. His later “epigenetic landscape” (a ball rolling down branching valleys) is still the field’s favorite metaphor.

1958

David Nanney gives the first formal systems definition: “epigenetic control systems” that maintain differentiated states above the level of DNA sequence.

1975

Robin Holliday & John Pugh, and independently Arthur Riggs, propose the mechanism: DNA methylation — a chemical tag copied by enzymes at each division — could be the heritable on/off switch development needed.

2007–09

Adrian Bird and the Berger et al. consensus sharpen the modern operational definition used throughout this page: heritable changes in gene expression or chromatin state, with no change to the DNA sequence itself.

1942

Waddington CH. “The epigenotype.” Endeavour 1:18–20.

The founding concept: development is driven by an “epigenotype” — interactions among genes and their products — sitting between genotype and phenotype. The original paper predates DOIs, so it carries none; a 2012 reprint exists in the International Journal of Epidemiology, but sources disagree on that reprint’s DOI, so the original is the safe citation.

No DOI on the original
1958

Nanney DL. “Epigenetic control systems.” Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA 44:712–717.

Working from ciliate biology, Nanney argued cells need two kinds of systems: genetic systems that store information, and epigenetic systems that decide which of it is expressed and maintain that decision — the first rigorous framing of epigenetics as regulation layered on top of sequence.

doi.org/10.1073/pnas.44.7.712 PMC528665 · free full text
1975

Holliday R, Pugh JE. “DNA modification mechanisms and gene activity during development.” Science 187:226–232.

The methylation hypothesis: enzymes could methylate specific DNA sites to switch genes off, and a maintenance enzyme could copy the pattern to daughter strands — giving development a molecular memory. Remarkably specific, and essentially right.

doi.org/10.1126/science.1111098
1975

Riggs AD. “X inactivation, differentiation, and DNA methylation.” Cytogenetics and Cell Genetics 14:9–25.

Published the same year, independently: Riggs proposed methylation as the mechanism that locks in X-chromosome inactivation and other differentiated states, and predicted the maintenance methyltransferase that was later found (DNMT1).

doi.org/10.1159/000130315
2007

Bird A. “Perceptions of epigenetics.” Nature 447:396–398.

A field leader pushes back on concept creep: epigenetics should keep its core criterion — a structural adaptation of chromatin that registers, signals, or perpetuates an altered activity state — rather than becoming a synonym for “any regulation.” Worth reading before the myths section below.

doi.org/10.1038/nature05913
2009

Berger SL, Kouzarides T, Shiekhattar R, Shilatifard A. “An operational definition of epigenetics.” Genes & Development 23:781–783.

The consensus definition quoted at the top of this page, hammered out at a Cold Spring Harbor meeting: stably heritable phenotypes from changes in a chromosome that do not alter the DNA sequence.

doi.org/10.1101/gad.1787609

Section 02

Core mechanisms: writers, erasers, readers

Four systems do most of the work. DNA methylation tags the DNA itself. Histone modifications tag the proteins DNA wraps around. Chromatin remodeling and 3D folding control physical access to genes. Non-coding RNAs guide the other three to their targets. Each runs on the same logic: enzymes that write marks, enzymes that erase them, and proteins that read them.

Mechanism ADNA methylation — the original mark

The best-understood epigenetic mark is a methyl group (–CH3) attached to cytosine, almost always where a C is followed by a G — a CpG site. Methylation at a gene’s promoter usually silences it. DNMT3A and DNMT3B lay down new patterns (de novo), DNMT1 copies them onto new DNA at every division (maintenance), and the TET enzymes can remove them by oxidizing 5-methylcytosine (5mC) to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) — the discovery that demethylation is an active process, not just dilution.

1987

Gardiner-Garden M, Frommer M. “CpG islands in vertebrate genomes.” Journal of Molecular Biology 196:261–282.

Defined CpG islands: roughly 1 kb stretches, rich in C and G, dense with CpG sites, and usually kept unmethylated — most sit on gene promoters, making them the genome’s regulatory front doors and the sites whose abnormal methylation matters in cancer (Section 5).

doi.org/10.1016/0022-2836(87)90689-9
1992

Li E, Bestor TH, Jaenisch R. “Targeted mutation of the DNA methyltransferase gene results in embryonic lethality.” Cell 69:915–926.

Knocking out Dnmt1 in mice is lethal mid-gestation: without the maintenance methyltransferase, methylation patterns collapse and development fails. Methylation is not decoration — embryos cannot develop without it.

doi.org/10.1016/0092-8674(92)90611-F
1999

Okano M, Bell DW, Haber DA, Li E. “DNA methyltransferases Dnmt3a and Dnmt3b are essential for de novo methylation and mammalian development.” Cell 99:247–257.

Identified DNMT3A and DNMT3B as the de novo writers that establish new methylation patterns in the early embryo; losing Dnmt3b is embryonic lethal. Together with Li 1992, this completes the writer/maintenance division of labor.

doi.org/10.1016/S0092-8674(00)81656-6
2009

Tahiliani M, et al. “Conversion of 5-methylcytosine to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine in mammalian DNA by MLL partner TET1.” Science 324:930–935.

The eraser, found: TET1 oxidizes 5mC to 5hmC. Before this paper, demethylation was assumed to be passive (lost by dilution as cells divide); Tahiliani showed cells actively remove the mark — and that 5hmC is abundant in its own right, especially in neurons.

doi.org/10.1126/science.1170116 PubMed 19372391 PMC2715015 · free full text
2010

Ito S, et al. “Role of Tet proteins in 5mC to 5hmC conversion, ES-cell self-renewal and inner cell mass specification.” Nature 466:1129–1133.

Showed Tet1-driven 5mC-to-5hmC conversion is required for embryonic stem cells to maintain themselves and to specify the inner cell mass — tying the demethylation machinery directly to the earliest cell-fate decisions.

doi.org/10.1038/nature09303

Mechanism BHistone modifications — the “histone code”

DNA spools around histone proteins, and their tails accept a zoo of chemical tags — acetyl, methyl, phosphate, ubiquitin groups — that loosen or tighten the spool and recruit other machinery. Acetylation (added by HATs, removed by HDACs) generally opens chromatin; methylation is context-dependent: H3K4me3 marks active promoters, H3K27me3 marks Polycomb-repressed genes, H3K9me3 marks constitutive heterochromatin. Strahl & Allis proposed these combine into a readable “code”; how literally the cell “reads” combinations is still debated (see Ptashne’s critique in Section 8).

1996

Brownell JE, et al. “Tetrahymena histone acetyltransferase A: a homolog to yeast Gcn5 linking histone acetylation to gene activation.” Cell 84:843–851.

The first histone acetyltransferase (HAT) identified — and it turned out to be a known transcriptional activator, Gcn5. That single result fused two fields: the enzymes that tag histones are the machinery that switches genes on.

doi.org/10.1016/S0092-8674(00)81063-6
1996

Taunton J, Hassig CA, Schreiber SL. “A mammalian histone deacetylase related to the yeast transcriptional regulator Rpd3p.” Science 272:408–411.

Cloned the first mammalian histone deacetylase, HDAC1 — the eraser opposing the HATs. Two decades later, HDAC inhibitors became approved cancer drugs (Section 5); this is where that pharmacology starts.

doi.org/10.1126/science.272.5260.408 PubMed 8602529
2000

Strahl BD, Allis CD. “The language of covalent histone modifications.” Nature 403:41–45.

The “histone code” hypothesis: combinations of tail modifications, not single marks, constitute a language read by effector proteins to produce distinct outcomes. One of the most influential — and most argued-over — short papers in the field.

doi.org/10.1038/47412 PubMed 10638745
2001

Jenuwein T, Allis CD. “Translating the histone code.” Science 293:1074–1080.

Gave the code its first concrete writer–reader pair for methylation: the Suv39h enzymes write H3K9 methylation and HP1 reads it to build heterochromatin — proof that histone methylation is a signaling system, not just scaffolding.

doi.org/10.1126/science.1063127
2006

Bernstein BE, et al. “A bivalent chromatin structure marks key developmental genes in embryonic stem cells.” Cell 125:315–326.

Key developmental genes in stem cells carry both the activating H3K4me3 and repressive H3K27me3 marks — “bivalent domains” that hold genes silent but poised, ready to resolve either way when the cell differentiates. A landmark of stem-cell epigenetics.

doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2006.02.041 PubMed 16630819

Mechanism CChromatin remodeling & the 3D genome

Marks are only half the story — genes also have to be physically reachable. ATP-driven remodelers slide nucleosomes around, and the whole chromosome folds into compartments and topologically associating domains (TADs): megabase-scale neighborhoods that keep enhancers and their target genes in contact and insulate them from neighbors. Two consortium projects, ENCODE and the Roadmap Epigenomics Project, mapped this regulatory layer across human cell types.

2002

Dekker J, et al. “Capturing chromosome conformation.” Science 295:1306–1311.

Invented chromosome conformation capture (3C), the first method to measure which DNA segments touch each other inside an intact nucleus — the technique from which the entire 3D-genome field (4C, 5C, Hi-C) descends.

doi.org/10.1126/science.1067799
2009

Lieberman-Aiden E, et al. “Comprehensive mapping of long-range interactions reveals folding principles of the human genome.” Science 326:289–293.

Hi-C: genome-wide contact mapping in one experiment. Revealed that the genome segregates into open (active) and closed (inactive) compartments and folds as a “fractal globule” — densely packed yet knot-free, so any locus can be pulled out and read.

doi.org/10.1126/science.1181369
2012

Dixon JR, et al. “Topological domains in mammalian genomes identified by analysis of chromatin interactions.” Nature 485:376–380.

Discovered TADs — self-interacting megabase domains, largely conserved between cell types and species, that organize regulation. Disrupting TAD boundaries can rewire enhancers onto the wrong genes, a mechanism now implicated in developmental disorders and cancer.

doi.org/10.1038/nature11082
2012

ENCODE Project Consortium. “An integrated encyclopedia of DNA elements in the human genome.” Nature 489:57–74.

The ENCODE flagship paper: biochemical activity mapped across 147 cell types, with some activity assigned to ~80% of the genome — a claim about “function” that drew sharp criticism and is best read as a map of activity, not proof of purpose. Either way, it remains the reference annotation of the regulatory genome.

doi.org/10.1038/nature11247 PubMed 22955616 PMC3439153 · free full text
2015

Roadmap Epigenomics Consortium, et al. “Integrative analysis of 111 reference human epigenomes.” Nature 518:317–330.

Reference maps of histone marks, chromatin accessibility, and DNA methylation across 111 human epigenomes — and a demonstration that disease-associated genetic variants (from GWAS) concentrate in regulatory elements active in the relevant cell type. The bridge between epigenomics and human genetics.

doi.org/10.1038/nature14248 PubMed 25693563 PMC4530010 · free full text

Mechanism DNon-coding RNAs — the guides

RNAs that never become protein can still run epigenetic programs: they recruit silencing machinery to specific addresses. Three classic cases: Xist, the long non-coding RNA that shuts down one X chromosome in female mammals; genomic imprinting, where methylation marks a gene’s parental origin so only the mother’s or father’s copy is used; and RNA-directed DNA methylation in plants, where small RNAs tell DNMTs exactly where to methylate.

1996

Penny GD, et al. “Requirement for Xist in X chromosome inactivation.” Nature 379:131–137.

Proved the Xist RNA is required for X-chromosome inactivation: delete it, and the chromosome can no longer be silenced. Xist coats the chromosome it is made from and recruits the silencing machinery — the paradigmatic epigenetic lncRNA.

doi.org/10.1038/379131a0 PubMed 8538762
1991

Bartolomei MS, Zemel S, Tilghman SM. “Parental imprinting of the mouse H19 gene.” Nature 351:153–155.

A founding imprinting paper: the mouse H19 gene is expressed from only one parental allele, with the silent copy carrying allele-specific methylation. Your cells know which parent each chromosome came from — and methylation is how they remember.

doi.org/10.1038/351153a0
1993

Ferguson-Smith AC, et al. Nature 362:751–755.Title not cross-checked

Demonstrated parent-of-origin-specific DNA methylation controlling imprinting at the Igf2/H19 region — the growth-factor gene and its neighboring lncRNA are regulated as a unit by methylation marks that track each chromosome’s parental origin. (Citation details per multiple independent reference lists; the paper’s exact title was not cross-checked during this research.)

doi.org/10.1038/362751a0
1994

Wassenegger M, Heimes S, Riedel L, Sänger HL. “RNA-directed de novo methylation of genomic sequences in plants.” Cell 76:567–576.

First demonstration that RNA can direct de novo methylation of matching genomic DNA — the discovery behind RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM), the plant immune system against transposons and viruses, and still the cleanest molecular route by which an RNA signal becomes a heritable DNA mark.

doi.org/10.1016/0092-8674(94)90119-8

Section 03

Landmark in-vivo studies: environment writes on the genome

Mechanisms are chemistry; these are the whole-animal and human studies that made epigenetics famous — proof that diet, maternal behavior, famine, and social caste can move methylation, and that the marks can last a lifetime. Read them carefully, though: almost all show effects within a lifetime or in directly exposed offspring — which is not the same as inheritance across generations (that harder claim is Section 7).

The Agouti mouse — nutrition you can see

The Agouti viable yellow (Avy) mouse carries a retrotransposon inserted next to the agouti coat-color gene. When the insert is unmethylated, the mouse is yellow, obese, and diabetes-prone; when methylated, brown and lean — same DNA, different epigenetic state, visible at a glance. It became the field’s demonstration animal for nutritional epigenetics.

2003

Waterland RA, Jirtle RL. “Transposable elements: targets for early nutritional effects on epigenetic gene regulation.” Molecular and Cellular Biology 23:5293–5300.

Feeding pregnant Avy mice a diet rich in methyl donors (folate, vitamin B12, choline, betaine) shifted their pups toward brown coats by increasing methylation at the retrotransposon — the classic demonstration that early nutrition retunes the epigenome with lifelong, visible consequences.

doi.org/10.1128/MCB.23.15.5293-5300.2003
2007

Dolinoy DC, Huang D, Jirtle RL. “Maternal nutrient supplementation counteracts bisphenol A-induced DNA hypomethylation in early development.” Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA 104:13056–13061.

The flip side: exposing pregnant mice to bisphenol A (BPA) reduced methylation at Avy and shifted pups toward yellow — and maternal methyl-donor supplementation counteracted it. Nutrition could buffer an environmental epigenetic insult, at least in this model system.

doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0703739104

Maternal care and the stress response — rats, then humans

Michael Meaney’s group showed that how much a rat mother licks and grooms her pups calibrates their stress system for life — through methylation of the glucocorticoid receptor gene in the hippocampus. Five years later, the human analogue appeared in postmortem brain tissue.

2004

Weaver ICG, et al. “Epigenetic programming by maternal behavior.” Nature Neuroscience 7:847–854.

Pups of high-licking/grooming mothers had less methylation at the hippocampal glucocorticoid-receptor (Nr3c1) promoter, more receptor expression, and calmer adult stress responses. Cross-fostering swapped the outcome with the rearing mother — proving nurture, not sequence — and HDAC inhibition could reverse the marks in adults.

doi.org/10.1038/nn1276 PubMed 15220929
2009

McGowan PO, et al. “Epigenetic regulation of the glucocorticoid receptor in human brain associates with childhood abuse.” Nature Neuroscience 12:342–348.

The human follow-up: in postmortem hippocampus, suicide victims with a history of childhood abuse showed hypermethylation of the NR3C1 promoter and reduced receptor expression compared with non-abused suicide victims and controls — the same gene, the same direction of effect as the rat work. Small samples and postmortem tissue mean it is suggestive, not definitive.

doi.org/10.1038/nn.2270

The Dutch Hunger Winter — famine written in methylation, 60 years on

In the winter of 1944–45, the western Netherlands starved under Nazi blockade; rations fell below 1,000 kcal/day. Because exposure dates are known almost to the week and birth records survived, the cohort born around the famine is epidemiology’s natural experiment in prenatal environment — and the first strong human evidence that early-gestation conditions leave methylation differences detectable in old age.

2008

Heijmans BT, et al. “Persistent epigenetic differences associated with prenatal exposure to famine in humans.” Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA 105:17046–17049.

People exposed to famine in early gestation had lower methylation at the imprinted IGF2 gene some six decades later than their unexposed same-sex siblings; exposure late in gestation showed no such effect. Timing — the periconceptional window, when epigenetic patterns are being set — was everything.

doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0806560105 PubMed 18955703
2009

Tobi EW, et al. “DNA methylation differences after exposure to prenatal famine are common and timing- and sex-specific.” Human Molecular Genetics 18:4046–4053.

The genome-wide extension: famine-associated methylation differences were common rather than confined to IGF2, clustered at growth and metabolism genes, and depended on both the timing of exposure and the sex of the exposed fetus.

doi.org/10.1093/hmg/ddp353
2014

Tobi EW, et al. “DNA methylation signatures link prenatal famine exposure to growth and metabolism.” Nature Communications 5:5592.

Later follow-up tying the famine-associated methylation signatures to adult growth and metabolic phenotypes — moving the cohort’s story from “marks persist” toward “marks track the outcomes the cohort is known for.”

doi.org/10.1038/ncomms6592

Read it precisely

The Hunger Winter findings are within-lifetime effects: the exposed individuals were themselves in the womb during the famine. They demonstrate durable developmental programming — not transmission to grandchildren, which is the separate, much weaker claim examined in Section 7.

Honeybees and twins — caste and drift

2008

Kucharski R, et al. “Nutritional control of reproductive status in honeybees via DNA methylation.” Science 319:1827–1830.

Queen and worker bees share a genome; diet decides. Silencing Dnmt3 in larvae produced queen-like development — ovaries and all — mimicking royal-jelly feeding. DNA methylation is the switch through which nutrition builds two radically different animals from identical DNA.

doi.org/10.1126/science.1153069 PubMed 18339900
2005

Fraga MF, et al. “Epigenetic differences arise during the lifetime of monozygotic twins.” Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA 102:10604–10609.

Young identical twins are epigenetically near-identical; older twins — especially those who lived apart and had different lifestyles — diverge substantially in DNA methylation and histone acetylation. “Epigenetic drift” over a lifetime helps explain why identical twins grow less alike, and discordant for disease, with age.

doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0500398102 PubMed 16009939

Section 04

Epigenetic clocks: methylation as a measure of age

Of everything methylation does, its most uncanny property is keeping time. A few hundred CpG sites, read from a blood sample, predict chronological age within a few years — and the gap between methylation age and calendar age predicts disease and death. Three generations of clocks refined what “age” they measure, and partial reprogramming experiments now ask whether the clock can be run backward.

Generation 1 · Chronological

Horvath & Hannum (2013)

Trained purely to predict calendar age. Horvath’s pan-tissue clock uses 353 CpGs and works across almost any tissue; Hannum’s 71-CpG clock is blood-based.

Generation 2 · Mortality risk

PhenoAge (2018) · GrimAge (2019)

Trained on clinical biomarkers, plasma proteins, and smoking history rather than age itself — so they predict lifespan and healthspan better than the first generation.

Generation 3 · Pace of aging

DunedinPACE (2022)

Trained on longitudinal decline across organ systems in a birth cohort — a speedometer rather than an odometer, designed to respond within intervention trials.

2013

Horvath S. “DNA methylation age of human tissues and cell types.” Genome Biology 14:R115.

The paper that launched the field: 353 CpG sites predict age across nearly all human tissues and cell types, and the discrepancy between methylation age and chronological age tracks health and mortality. Still the reference clock.

doi.org/10.1186/gb-2013-14-10-r115 PubMed 24138928 PMC4015143 · free full text
2013

Hannum G, et al. “Genome-wide methylation profiles reveal quantitative views of human aging rates.” Molecular Cell 49:359–367.

Published almost simultaneously: a 71-CpG blood clock, with the early demonstration that methylation-predicted age relates to all-cause mortality — the hint that clocks measure something more than birthdays.

doi.org/10.1016/j.molcel.2012.10.016
2018

Levine ME, et al. “An epigenetic biomarker of aging for lifespan and healthspan.” Aging 10:573–591.

PhenoAge: instead of training on calendar age, Levine trained on a “phenotypic age” built from clinical biomarkers — and got a clock that predicts mortality and disease better than purely chronological ones. The template for second-generation clocks.

doi.org/10.18632/aging.101414 PubMed 29676998
2019

Lu AT, et al. “DNA methylation GrimAge strongly predicts lifespan and healthspan.” Aging 11:303–327.

GrimAge — named, cheerfully, for the Grim Reaper — combines methylation surrogates for smoking pack-years and several plasma proteins. It became the strongest mortality predictor among the first- and second-generation clocks and the one most often used as a trial endpoint.

doi.org/10.18632/aging.101684
2022

Belsky DW, et al. “DunedinPACE, a DNA methylation biomarker of the pace of aging.” eLife 11:e73420.

A different question: not “how old are you?” but “how fast are you aging right now?” DunedinPACE was trained on two decades of multi-organ decline in the Dunedin birth cohort and can, in principle, show within a trial whether an intervention is slowing the pace — which static clocks cannot.

doi.org/10.7554/eLife.73420
2016

Ocampo A, et al. “In vivo amelioration of age-associated hallmarks by partial reprogramming.” Cell 167:1719–1733.e12.

The founding partial-reprogramming experiment: cyclic, short pulses of Yamanaka factors (OSKM) in progeroid mice reset epigenetic marks of age, extended lifespan, and improved tissue function — without pushing cells all the way back to pluripotency, which causes tumors. The result launched an entire longevity-biotech sector.

doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2016.11.052

Handle with care

Clocks are superb biomarkers — correlations engineered for prediction. Whether methylation-age acceleration causes aging, or merely records it, is unresolved; Section 9 covers the strongest causal claim (Yang et al. 2023) and why it remains debated. A supplement that moves a clock has not thereby been shown to extend life.

Section 05

Disease: cancer epigenetics, working drugs, and exposure fingerprints

Cancer is where epigenetics first paid clinical rent. Tumors routinely silence tumor-suppressor genes by methylation rather than mutation — and because methylation is reversible, it is druggable. Two classes of epigenetic drugs are now standard care in blood cancers, and one methylation test directly decides chemotherapy in brain tumors.

Cancer: silencing without mutation

1999

Toyota M, et al. “CpG island methylator phenotype in colorectal cancer.” Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences USA 96:8681–8686.

Defined the CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP): a subset of colorectal cancers that hypermethylate many CpG islands in a coordinated way — an epigenetic subclass of cancer, silencing genes wholesale without mutating them.

doi.org/10.1073/pnas.96.15.8681 PubMed 10411935 PMC17576 · free full text
2005

Hegi ME, et al. “MGMT gene silencing and benefit from temozolomide in glioblastoma.” New England Journal of Medicine 352:997–1003.

The landmark clinical biomarker: glioblastoma patients whose MGMT promoter is methylated — silencing a DNA-repair gene — benefit substantially from temozolomide chemotherapy; those without the mark largely do not. MGMT methylation testing remains standard in neuro-oncology.

doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa043331
2012

The Cancer Genome Atlas Network. “Comprehensive molecular portraits of human breast tumours.” Nature 490:61–70.

TCGA’s integrated portrait of 825 breast tumors — mutations, copy number, expression, and DNA methylation together — defined molecular subtypes that no single data type could see, establishing multi-omic (genetic plus epigenetic) classification as the norm in cancer biology.

doi.org/10.1038/nature11412

Approved epigenetic drugs — and the trials behind them

Two drug classes dominate: DNMT inhibitors (azacitidine, decitabine), which strip methylation and reactivate silenced genes, used mainly in myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS); and HDAC inhibitors (vorinostat, romidepsin), which loosen chromatin, used in cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL). Their effects are real but modest — proof of principle that the epigenome is druggable, not a finished success story.

DrugClassPivotal evidence
AzacitidineVidaza DNMT inhibitor Silverman et al., J Clin Oncol 2002 — randomized trial establishing azacitidine in MDS; Fenaux et al., Lancet Oncol 2009 — extended overall survival vs. conventional care in higher-risk MDS.
DecitabineDacogen DNMT inhibitor Kantarjian et al., Cancer 2006 — phase III trial supporting decitabine in MDS.
VorinostatZolinza HDAC inhibitor Duvic et al., Blood 2007 — phase 2 trial in refractory cutaneous T-cell lymphoma.
RomidepsinIstodax HDAC inhibitor Piekarz et al., J Clin Oncol 2009 — phase II multi-institutional trial as monotherapy for CTCL.

EWAS: exposures leave readable fingerprints

Epigenome-wide association studies (EWAS) scan methylation across the genome for exposure signatures — the epigenetic answer to GWAS. The field’s exemplar is smoking, whose methylation signature is so strong and specific that it doubles as an objective smoking-history test.

2016

Joehanes R, et al. “Epigenetic signatures of cigarette smoking.” Circulation: Cardiovascular Genetics 9:436–447.

A blood EWAS of roughly 16,000 people: thousands of CpGs differ between smokers and non-smokers, the strongest at the AHRR gene, and many revert toward never-smoker levels after cessation — while some persist for decades. The template for reading environmental exposures off the epigenome, and a standing warning that EWAS signals are often consequences of exposure rather than causes of disease.

doi.org/10.1161/CIRCGENETICS.116.001506 PubMed 27651444

Section 06

Epigenome editing: rewriting the marks, not the letters

If methylation and histone marks control genes, can we edit the marks directly — leaving the DNA sequence untouched? The trick is a dead Cas9 (dCas9): it still homes to a guide-RNA-specified address but cannot cut, so it serves as a delivery vehicle for epigenetic enzymes. Fuse it to p300 and you acetylate one enhancer; to DNMT3A, you methylate one promoter; to TET1, you demethylate it. The 2021 CRISPRoff paper showed a single treatment can write a silencing program the cell then maintains on its own.

2015

Hilton IB, et al. “Epigenome editing by a CRISPR-Cas9-based acetyltransferase activates genes from promoters and enhancers.” Nature Biotechnology 33:510–517.

dCas9 fused to the p300 acetyltransferase core: targeted H3K27 acetylation switched on endogenous genes from their promoters and — the important part — from distant enhancers, proving locus-specific epigenome editing could activate, not just repress.

doi.org/10.1038/nbt.3199 PubMed 25849900 PMC4430400 · free full text
2016

Liu XS, et al. “Editing DNA methylation in the mammalian genome.” Cell 167:233–247.e17.

Both directions of methylation editing in one paper: dCas9–DNMT3A to write methylation at a chosen locus, dCas9–TET1 to erase it — with the edited states inherited through cell division in mammalian cells.

doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2016.08.056
2016

Vojta A, et al. “Repurposing the CRISPR-Cas9 system for targeted DNA methylation.” Nucleic Acids Research 44:5615–5628.

An independent dCas9–DNMT3A system for targeted methylation, published the same year — useful corroboration that the approach worked across labs, and a practical methods reference for the tool-builders who followed.

doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkw159
2016

Morita S, et al. “Targeted DNA demethylation in vivo using dCas9-peptide repeat and scFv-TET1 catalytic domain fusions.” Nature Biotechnology 34:1060–1065.

Moved editing into living animals: a SunTag scaffold amplifies dCas9’s signal by recruiting many TET1 catalytic domains to one address, achieving targeted demethylation in mice — the step that made therapeutic editing plausible rather than a cell-culture curiosity.

doi.org/10.1038/nbt.3658 PubMed 27571369
2021

Nuñez JK, et al. “Genome-wide programmable transcriptional memory by CRISPR-based epigenome editing.” Cell 184:2503–2519.e17.

CRISPRoff: one transient dose of a dCas9–KRAB–DNMT3A/3L fusion writes H3K9me3 plus DNA methylation that silences the target gene heritably — maintained through many divisions and stem-cell differentiation after the editor itself is gone. CRISPRon (dCas9–TET1) reverses it. Durable gene control with no DNA cut is the conceptual basis for the therapy now in human trials (Section 9).

doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2021.03.025 PubMed 33838111

Section 07

The inheritance debate: what crosses generations, and what doesn’t

This is the most contested claim in the field, so precision matters. Intergenerational effects — a pregnant mother’s exposure also directly exposes her fetus and the fetus’s own germ cells — are well documented (Sections 3 and 5). Transgenerational inheritance means transmission to generations never exposed at all (F3 and beyond, for a maternal exposure), via the gametes alone. In plants and in the worm C. elegans, that is established fact. In mammals, two waves of germline reprogramming erase most methylation, and the evidence is a genuine fight — laid out below, both sides, from the literature itself.

Start with the framing reviews: Heard & Martienssen, Cell 2014 (“Transgenerational epigenetic inheritance: myths and mechanisms,” free at PMC) set the terms of the debate and identify reprogramming as the central barrier; Daxinger & Whitelaw, Nature Reviews Genetics 2012 conclude that convincing mammalian cases via the gametes are rare and mechanistically thin; and Fitz-James & Cavalli, Nature Reviews Genetics 2022 survey the candidate carriers — small RNAs, histones, methylation — and still find the mammalian evidence contested.

The case for

2011

Rechavi O, Minevich G, Hobert O. “Transgenerational inheritance of an acquired small RNA-based antiviral response in C. elegans.” Cell 147:1248–1256.

Worms that survived a viral infection passed antiviral small RNAs — and the immunity itself — to descendants for multiple generations. One of the cleanest demonstrations of acquired-trait inheritance anywhere in biology.

doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2011.10.042
1999

Cubas P, Vincent C, Coen E. “An epigenetic mutation responsible for natural variation in floral symmetry.” Nature 400:157–161.DOI not cross-checked

In toadflax (Linaria), a naturally occurring hypermethylated epiallele of the Lcyc gene changes flower symmetry and is transmitted through meiosis — a wild, naturally evolved epimutation. Plants, lacking a sequestered germline, transmit such epialleles readily.

doi.org/10.1038/23800
2005

Anway MD, Cupp AS, Uzumcu M, Skinner MK. “Epigenetic transgenerational actions of endocrine disruptors and male fertility.” Science 308:1466–1469.

The founding mammalian claim: exposing gestating rats to the fungicide vinclozolin produced male-fertility defects transmitted through the male germline to F4, alongside altered sperm methylation. Hugely influential — and the claim the rebuttals at right directly target.

doi.org/10.1126/science.1108190
2006

Rassoulzadegan M, et al. “RNA-mediated non-mendelian inheritance of an epigenetic change in the mouse.” Nature 441:469–474.

Paramutation in a mammal: a white-tail phenotype in Kit mice was transmitted non-Mendelianly, and injecting RNA from mutant sperm into normal zygotes reproduced it — evidence that sperm RNA itself can carry an epigenetic state.

doi.org/10.1038/nature04674
2014

Dias BG, Ressler KJ. “Parental olfactory experience influences behavior and neural structure in subsequent generations.” Nature Neuroscience 17:89–96.

Mice fear-conditioned to an odor produced offspring and grand-offspring with heightened sensitivity to that specific odor and enlarged corresponding olfactory structures, with hypomethylation at the odorant-receptor gene in sperm. Striking — and frequently cited by critics as difficult to reconcile with reprogramming.

doi.org/10.1038/nn.3594
2014

Gapp K, et al. “Implication of sperm RNAs in transgenerational inheritance of the effects of early trauma in mice.” Nature Neuroscience 17:667–669.

Early-life trauma in male mice altered sperm microRNAs; injecting those RNAs into normal zygotes recapitulated the behavioral phenotype in the offspring. Functional evidence for sperm RNA as a carrier — note, though, that this demonstrates a paternal/intergenerational effect, not F3+ persistence.

doi.org/10.1038/nn.3695

The case against — and the replication record

2015

Iqbal K, et al. “Deleterious effects of endocrine disruptors are corrected in the mammalian germline by epigenome reprogramming.” Genome Biology 16:59.

The direct rebuttal: vinclozolin, BPA, and DEHP did alter methylation and transcription in exposed (G1) prospermatogonia — but the changes did not persist into G2, imprinting stayed normal, and reprogramming cleaned them up. A failure-to-persist result aimed squarely at Anway 2005.

doi.org/10.1186/s13059-015-0619-z PubMed 25853433 PMC4376074 · free full text
2015

Nadeau JH (with Whitelaw E). “The nature of evidence for and against epigenetic inheritance.” Genome Biology 16:137.

The accompanying commentary situates Iqbal as “important negative evidence” and lays out what a convincing mammalian demonstration would actually require — purified germ cells, exclusion of genetic confounds, persistence past the last exposed generation.

doi.org/10.1186/s13059-015-0709-y PMC4499204 · free full text
2014

Radford EJ, et al. “In utero undernourishment perturbs the adult sperm methylome and intergenerational metabolism.” Science 345:1255903.DOI not cross-checked

Often miscited as transgenerational proof: undernourishing pregnant mice altered the adult sperm methylome of their sons and the metabolism of the next generation — but as an intergenerational (F1/F2) effect, where direct exposure can never be excluded. No F3 persistence was shown.

doi.org/10.1126/science.1255903

The mechanistic problem

Mammals erase methylation twice: once in the primordial germ cells and again after fertilization. Any inherited mark must survive both resets. Imprinted genes and some retrotransposons are known escapees — which is why the credible mammalian claims cluster there — but a general-purpose channel for acquired marks has not been demonstrated.

Bottom line

Transgenerational epigenetic inheritance is firmly established in plants (RdDM-maintained epialleles) and C. elegans (germline small RNAs persisting for generations). In mammals, intergenerational and paternal effects replicate — sperm-RNA experiments especially — but durable F3+ inheritance of environmentally induced phenotypes faces prominent negative results and skeptical reviews, and there is no well-replicated human case. Anyone selling you “your grandmother’s trauma is methylated into your genome” is ahead of the evidence.

Section 08

Myths vs. reality

Epigenetics attracts hype because it sounds like it dissolves the nature/nurture divide. It doesn’t. The sharpest corrective comes from inside molecular biology: Mark Ptashne’s “Epigenetics: core misconcept” (PNAS 2013, free at PMC) argues that development is driven primarily by sequence-specific transcription factors, and that chromatin modifications are largely consequences of gene regulation recruited by those factors — not an autonomous causal layer above the genome. Adrian Bird’s “Perceptions of epigenetics” (Nature 2007) makes the companion point: the causal role of chromatin marks is routinely overstated in popular coverage.

Not implied · 1

It is not a Lamarckian revival

Even where acquired marks are transmitted — worms, plants — they typically fade within a few generations and never rewrite the genetic code. Mammals run dedicated reprogramming waves precisely to erase them. Giraffes’ necks are safe.

Not implied · 2

It does not mean genes don’t matter

Human trait heritability remains dominated by DNA sequence. Epigenetic marks explain a fraction of phenotypic variance, are themselves often under genetic control (meQTLs), and usually act downstream of sequence.

Not implied · 3

Acquired traits are not freely inherited in humans

There is no well-replicated case of F3+ transmission of an environmentally induced phenotype in humans. The famous human studies — famine, twins — concern within-lifetime programming or sibling comparisons, not grandchild inheritance.

Not implied · 4

“Epigenetic” does not mean “inherited”

Most epigenetic regulation operates within one lifetime: development, cell differentiation, responses to environment. Heritability across generations is the exceptional claim and needs exceptional evidence.

Reading epigenetics news critically — five questions

  • Correlation or cause? Was the mark shown to cause the phenotype — e.g., editing it changed the outcome — or does it merely travel with it? Most EWAS hits are passengers.
  • Were genetic confounds excluded? Methylation is partly controlled by nearby DNA variants (meQTLs) and by strain background. A “purely epigenetic” effect can be a genetic effect in disguise.
  • Which generation, exactly? For a maternal exposure, F1 (fetus) and F2 (the fetus’s germ cells) were directly exposed. Only F3+ counts as transgenerational — check which generation the headline is actually about.
  • Was it in germ cells, and did it survive reprogramming? A mark in blood or liver says nothing about transmission. The claim needs purified gametes and persistence past the erasure waves.
  • Has anyone replicated it? The mammalian inheritance literature is littered with single-lab results. Independent replication — and honest negative results like Iqbal 2015 — are the difference between a phenomenon and a press release.

Section 09

Open frontiers, 2023–2026

Four live directions: reading the epigenome one cell at a time, reading it in place in tissue, testing whether epigenetic information loss is a cause of aging (and reversible), and taking epigenetic editing into human patients for the first time.

Frontier ASingle-cell and spatial epigenomics

Bulk assays average millions of cells; the new methods resolve the epigenome per cell — and, with spatial techniques, per pixel of intact tissue.

2015

Buenrostro JD, et al. “Single-cell chromatin accessibility reveals principles of regulatory variation.” Nature 523:486–490.

The first robust single-cell ATAC-seq: chromatin accessibility measured cell by cell, showing that variation between cells is driven by specific trans-factors and mirrors 3D chromosome compartments. The methodological foundation of today’s single-cell epigenomic atlases, now routinely combined with RNA and protein readouts in multiome assays.

doi.org/10.1038/nature14590 PubMed 26083756 PMC4685948 · free full text
2022

Deng Y, et al. “Spatial-CUT&Tag: spatially resolved chromatin modification profiling at the cellular level.” Science 375:681–686.

Genome-wide histone-modification maps resolved pixel by pixel on frozen tissue sections — epigenomics without dissociating the tissue, preserving the spatial context that single-cell methods destroy.

doi.org/10.1126/science.abg7216 PubMed 35143307
2023

Zhang D, Deng Y, et al. “Spatial epigenome-transcriptome co-profiling of mammalian tissues.” Nature 616:113–122.DOI not cross-checked

Co-profiles the epigenome and the transcriptome in the same tissue section — joining the regulatory state to its gene-expression output, in place. (DOI given in standard citation form; not independently cross-checked during this research.)

doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-05795-1

Frontier BIs epigenetic information loss a cause of aging?

2023

Yang JH, et al. “Loss of epigenetic information as a cause of mammalian aging.” Cell 186:305–326.e27.

The “ICE” mouse: inducing DNA breaks that are repaired without mutation still scrambled epigenetic information, accelerated aging phenotypes, and advanced the methylation clocks — and OSK reprogramming reversed it. The boldest causal version of the “information theory of aging.” High-profile and debated; a 2024 correction (doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2024.01.049) added methodological detail.

doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2022.12.027 PubMed 36638792
2023

Yang JH, et al. “Chemically induced reprogramming to reverse cellular aging.” Aging 15:5966–5989.

Reprogramming without genes: six small-molecule cocktails reversed transcriptomic age in old and senescent human cells in under a week, without erasing cell identity. If it holds up, chemical partial reprogramming is far easier to deliver than viral OSK — the operative word being if.

doi.org/10.18632/aging.204896 PubMed 37437248 PMC10373966 · free full text

Frontier CEpigenetic editing reaches the clinic — EPI-321

The first epigenetic-editing therapy in human trials is EPI-321 (Epicrispr Biotechnologies, formerly Epic Bio), a one-time treatment for facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (FSHD) designed to durably silence the toxic DUX4 gene’s expression without altering the DNA sequence — CRISPRoff-style logic, in patients. A first-in-human Phase 1/2 trial is ongoing. At the 31st World Muscle Society congress (Hiroshima, October 2026), the company presented six-month data showing increases in lean muscle volume and strength in the first six treated patients against natural-history comparators, and announced an $8M CIRM grant to support the trial.

Preliminary — company-reported

Everything above about EPI-321 rests on company press releases, not peer-reviewed data, and no primary preclinical paper or trial-registry record was identified during this research. Treat the results as promising and unverified. Sources: Business Wire, Oct 19, 2026 (CIRM grant) · World Muscle Society 2026 six-month data release. Status current as of Oct 19, 2026; it will change.
