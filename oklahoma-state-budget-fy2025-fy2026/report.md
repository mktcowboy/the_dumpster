# Oklahoma State Budget Analysis: FY2025 vs FY2026

**Fiscal-year convention:** Oklahoma's FY runs July 1 – June 30.
- **FY2025** = July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025. Passed in the 2024 session (59th Legislature, 2nd regular session); general appropriations bill **SB1125**; signed June 2024; Gov. Kevin Stitt.
- **FY2026** = July 1, 2025 – June 30, 2026. Passed in the 2025 session (60th Legislature, 1st regular session); general appropriations bill **HB2766**; passed House 5/20/2025, Senate 5/22/2025 (27–19), became law **without the governor's signature on 5/29/2025**.

**A note on "the budget number" (important for charts):** Oklahoma sources report different top-line totals depending on what is counted: (a) the General Appropriations (GA) bill only, (b) GA bill + supplementals for the prior year enacted in the same session, (c) GA bill + one-time transfers to dedicated funds. The tables below give each figure with its accounting basis; the GA-bill-only figures are the only ones strictly comparable across years on identical agency line items.

## Summary

- **FY2025 headline total: $12.47B** (SB1125 "framework"; GA bill proper = $11.558B; + $267M FY2024 supplementals in the session package; OK Policy counts $12.2B of FY2025-budget spending). Largest nominal budget in state history at the time; agencies got ~2% increases overall.
- **FY2026 headline total: $12.59B** (+0.56% per House fiscal staff; GA bill proper = $11.593B, +0.30% on the same basis; OK Policy counts $12.08B + $783.5M FY2025 supplementals = $12.87B session total). Mostly flat agency budgets; growth concentrated in **Education (+$90M)** and **Health/OHCA (+$92M)**; declines concentrated in one-time capital items that rolled off (Tax Commission −$51M emission rebates; Aeronautics −$45M; Natural Resources −$75M).
- **Tax policy:** FY2025 cycle eliminated the state grocery sales tax (HB1955, eff. Aug 2024, −$418M/yr recurring — the year's big revenue hit). FY2026 cycle enacted a 0.25% income-tax cut with a trigger path toward full elimination (**HB2764**; full-year cost est. $340M in FY2027, $357M in FY2028). Stitt's proposed 0.5% cut was rejected by the legislature.
- **One-time spending** dominated both cycles: FY2025 directed ~$1.3B to capital funds (Legacy Capital $371.5M, Capital Assets Maintenance $350M, OCIA deferred maintenance $350M, RETRO $200M); FY2026 directed $1.006B (Lawton private-prison purchase $312M, OSU vet hospital $250M, OU pediatric heart hospital $200M, military facilities $41.6M, PREP projects $58M, ARPA/Statewide Recovery Fund $103M, ad valorem reimbursements $73.6M, FY25 agency supplementals $49.4M).
- **Savings:** record ~$4.7B headline reserves during FY2025 cycle; at the start of FY2026: $2.31B in the three big funds (Rainy Day $1.33B, Revenue Stabilization $425M, Rate Preservation $561M) plus $1.525B unspent GR cash. The legislature used one-time savings to subsidize the recurring income-tax cut.

## FY2025 narrative (July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025)

**Process & top line.** Budget agreement announced May 2024; **SB1125** (general appropriations) passed the Senate 31–11 on May 28, 2024 and the House that week; signed by Stitt in June 2024. Headline: **$12.47B**, the largest nominal state budget ever at the time; agencies received ~2% increases overall. Republicans touted record savings (~$4.7B); House Democrats argued money was left dormant. Democrats criticized a $50M increase (to a $150M cap) in private-school tax credits and funding shifts toward private prisons/business deals; Republicans highlighted deferred maintenance and infrastructure.

**Education ($5.6B, 46%).** SDE was *cut* $108M (−3%) while other education agencies grew: CareerTech +19.2% ($196M, incl. $28.59M skills-center expansion), Regents +$20M (+2%, $1.02B), OETA +102% ($5.8M, transmitters), Health Care Workforce Training Commission +35.8% ($10.4M). New SDE line items: +$25M formula, $2.5M teacher maternity leave, $2.65M paid student teaching, +$16.6M FBA.

**Health.** OHCA $1.3B — up 46.8% vs FY2024, but that is misleading: FY2024 was artificially low because of enhanced pandemic federal Medicaid match; vs FY2023 the increase was ~4%. OSDH +25% ($89M), OSUMA +22.4% ($95.9M), ODMHSAS +7.8% ($387M), Veterans Affairs −14.9% ($44M).

**Human services ($951M).** DHS +$31.6M (+4%) with DDSD waiver $3M and $45.5M provider-rate increases; Juvenile Affairs +$6.5M (+6%); Rehab Services +9.6%.

**Public safety ($1B).** DPS +20% ($126M); DOC $544M (−1.5%); AG +65% (+$25.3M) with $10M domestic violence + $18M sheriffs; Supreme Court +$13.4M (reclassification of the Family Representation and Advocacy Program from District Courts).

**Transportation/capital.** ODOT road funding +$16.2M industrial/lake access + $16.2M Tulsa ports; RETRO $200M to advance the 8-year road plan. The big story was one-time capital: new **Capital Assets Maintenance and Protection Fund ($350M)**, Legacy Capital Finance Revolving Fund ($177M → $371.5M in project allocations, 73% to OU/OSU), OCIA deferred maintenance ($350M, $125M in FY25 projects).

**Revenue & tax.** The Feb 2024 Board of Equalization certified only ~$8.274B GR + $175M federal with a thin **$72M** surplus. The session's landmark: **repeal of the state grocery sales tax (HB1955, effective Aug 2024)** — a **$418M/year recurring** revenue loss (largest tax break in state history; benefits skewed to high earners). Stitt's 0.25% income-tax cut push failed (est. cost $235–293M). FY2025 GR collections ultimately beat estimates by 2.6% on strong income and gross-production taxes; sales tax fell $314M as expected from the grocery repeal.

## FY2026 narrative (July 1, 2025 – June 30, 2026)

**Process & top line.** Budget deal struck among Stitt, House Speaker Hilbert and Senate President Pro Tem Paxton; **HB2766** (author: Rep. Trey Caldwell) passed the House 5/20/2025 and Senate 5/22/2025 (27–19); **became law without the governor's signature on 5/29/2025**. Headline **$12.59B** (+0.56% reported) — the narrowest of increases, reflecting tighter revenue. Democrats said it underfunded Medicaid, child care and agency requests ($800M more requested than offered); far-right Republicans said the $200M savings deposit should have been tax relief. Transparency regressed vs 2024: closed-door leadership deal, per OK Policy.

**Where it grew.** Education +$89.7M (+1.58%): SDE rebuilt to $3.983B (+3.15%), incl. +$25M to the school funding formula; Regents nearly flat at $1.098B. Health +$92.1M (+4.13%): OHCA $1.411B (+7.6%) — still only about half the $495M the agency requested to cover FMAP-driven Medicaid cost growth (the legislature also tapped $34.6M from the Rate Preservation Fund to hold provider rates). ODMHSAS +4.2% ($403.3M, + $27.4M FY25 supplemental). Human Services +2.5% ($975.4M); PS&J +0.8% ($1.046B, AG up another 8.4%).

**Where it shrank (mostly one-time roll-off).** GG&T −7.9%: Tax Commission −$51M (emission rebates ended), Aerospace −$45.4M (airport/engine-test-cell one-times ended), OMES −$9.3M. NRR −21.2%: Agriculture −$16.1M, Commerce −$17.7M, DEQ −$16.8M (parking garage), Water Board −$12.7M. ODOT −$21.1M (−2.5%). These cuts are overwhelmingly removals of FY2025 one-time capital, not base-budget cuts — consistent with the governor's "flat recurring budget" framing ($11.09B recurring base).

**Policy changes.** **HB2764**: 0.25% income-tax cut effective Nov. 1, 2025, with trigger-based quarter-point cuts toward eventual elimination; bottom three of six brackets eliminated. Full-year revenue cost est. **$340M (FY2027), $357M (FY2028)** — partially subsidized by drawing one-time savings ($38M Rainy Day → Special Cash). Stitt had proposed a deeper 0.5% "half and a path" cut ($202.6M + $37.4M corporate in FY26); the legislature chose the smaller cut. The grocery-tax repeal's full weight hit in FY2026 (−$418M recurring).

**One-time spending ($1.006B misc).** Lawton private-prison purchase $312M (SB1160, $238M from Revenue Stabilization Fund); OSU vet hospital $250M; OU pediatric heart hospital $200M; military facilities $41.6M; PREP 3.0 $58M; ARPA/Statewide Recovery $103M; opioid abatement $21.3M; ad valorem reimbursements $73.6M; FY25 agency supplementals ($22M SDE FBA, $27.4M ODMHSAS).

**Revenue & savings.** Feb 2025 BOE: ~$12.4B spending authority (up from $12.3B in Dec 2024) on stronger income and GPT collections. At FY2026 start: **$2.31B** in savings funds (Rainy Day $1.33B, Revenue Stabilization $425M, Rate Preservation $561M) + **$1.525B** unspent GR cash. June 2025 BOE revision raised authorized expenditure authority to $12.74B.

## FY2025 vs FY2026 — comparison

| Dimension | FY2025 | FY2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Headline total | $12.47B | $12.59B (+~1%) |
| GA-bill-only growth | — | +0.30% |
| Budget character | Record nominal budget; ~2% agency increases; capital-deployment year | Flat "status quo" budget; growth only where mandated (Medicaid, formula) |
| Education | $5.668B GA (SDE cut $108M) | $5.758B GA (+1.6%; SDE rebuilt +3.2%) |
| Health (OHCA) | $1.311B (FMAP unwind made +46.8% headline) | $1.411B (+7.6%; FMAP decline again the driver) |
| Signature tax change | Grocery sales tax repealed (−$418M/yr recurring) | Income tax cut 0.25% + elimination triggers (−$340M/yr full effect) |
| One-time flavor | Capital funds: CAMP $350M, OCIA $350M, LCFR/legacy $371.5M, RETRO $200M | Big-ticket acquisitions: prison $312M, vet hospital $250M, heart hospital $200M |
| Savings posture | ~$4.7B record headline reserves | $2.31B funds + $1.525B unspent cash; tapped one-time funds to fund recurring tax cut |
| Governor's role | Stitt signed; his income-tax push failed | HB2766 became law *without his signature*; legislature took the smaller tax cut he was offered |

**Key narrative differences.** FY2025 was a "surplus deployment" year: federal stimulus had padded reserves, the legislature banked record savings and sprayed one-time money across capital funds, while the grocery-tax repeal quietly removed $418M of recurring revenue. FY2026 was a "flat with landmines" year: the grocery repeal's full-year bite plus FMAP-driven Medicaid growth forced mostly-flat agency budgets, the income-tax cut was enacted on a trigger anyway and backfilled with one-time savings, and the one-time portfolio shifted from distributed capital funds to a few mega-projects (prison, hospitals). Both budgets papered over a structural mismatch — recurring revenue cuts funded with non-recurring money — that FY2027 was already showing (Feb 2026 BOE authorized 4.5% less authority).
