Public-Private Partnerships in U.S. Water Infrastructure, Cleanup and Restoration

Research notes on PPP models, U.S. examples, federal funding, restoration markets and RES (Resource Environmental Solutions)

Prepared November 5, 2026 · Numbered citations link to the reference list at the end

Key points at a glance
  • True P3s are rare in U.S. water. CBO estimates P3s accounted for 1–3% of highway, transit and water infrastructure spending since 1990, and water P3s have mostly been long-term operation and maintenance (O&M) contracts rather than private financing [1][2].
  • Federal money mostly flows through public channels. The Bipartisan Infrastructure Law (BIL) gives EPA more than $50 billion for water, including $10 billion for emerging contaminants such as PFAS, plus $3.5 billion for Superfund and $1.5 billion for brownfields [4]. WIFIA loans can reach private partners if a public sponsor backs the project [2][3].
  • The big concessions (Bayonne, Rialto) brought upfront cash and capital, but they locked in revenue requirements and investor returns that later pushed rates up [5][7][10]. Atlanta and Missoula show that cities can and do take systems back [8][15].
  • Restoration is where private delivery is most mature. Federal rules prefer mitigation banks [18], and firms like RES sell credits and deliver pay-for-performance nutrient and sediment reductions for state DOTs in the Chesapeake Bay states [26].

1. PPP models and how they apply to water

A PPP is a long-term contract that bundles some combination of design, construction, financing, operation and maintenance and shifts risk to a private party [1][2]. In U.S. water, the most common form is long-term contract operations. CBO found that almost all of the estimated $1.9 billion private water firms earned from municipal and federal P3-type contracts in 2016 was for O&M, with less than $100 million for design-build work [1]. Many drinking-water utilities are investor-owned outright, while wastewater systems are generally municipal [2].

2. U.S. landscape and notable examples

CBO counts water P3s as rare and links that to worries about fee increases, contracts that can't anticipate every contingency, and other ways to cut costs without giving up public control [1]. Some studies show O&M savings and better regulatory compliance, but CBO says the data come with enough caveats that clear conclusions are hard to draw [1].

Example projects (verified)

  • Bayonne, NJ (2012, 40-year concession). United Water (now Suez) and KKR paid $150 million upfront and committed about $157 million to system upgrades over the contract life [6]. Rates rose 8.5% at the start. Under a "Revenue Path" revenue guarantee, the city then had to raise rates 13.25% in 2016 instead of the roughly 3.5–4% it expected [5]. NJ.com reported rates up more than 50% by 2019 and cited a city statement that the investor was guaranteed an 11% annual return [7].
  • Atlanta, GA (1999–2003, 20-year O&M). At the time this was the largest U.S. water privatization. The city and United Water agreed to end it after about four years. A city audit found bill collections below the contract's 98.5% standard and accounts receivable more than doubled [8][9].
  • Rialto, CA (2012, 30-year concession). Led by Table Rock Capital, with Veolia as operator after a union campaign pushed out American Water. The deal raised about $177 million: $26 million in equity and $146 million in privately placed 30-year debt. Equity's pre-tax return requirement was 19.6% [10]. The city keeps ownership of the assets and pays a monthly fee that includes a fixed capital charge [11].
  • Pittsburgh, PA (Veolia management contract, ended 2015). In 2016 the Pittsburgh Water and Sewer Authority (PWSA) sued Veolia over its management, including a change in corrosion-control chemicals that was tied to the city's lead problems [12].
  • Flint, MI (consultant lessons). Veolia North America was a technical consultant, not a P3 operator. It settled with Michigan and about 26,000 residents for $53 million in February 2025 and denied liability [13]. The lesson is that limited advisory roles still carry public-health liability.
  • Claremont, NH (small-system O&M). H2O Innovation has run the 1.5 MGD water and wastewater plants since 2012 under successive five-year contracts [14]. Weakly sourced: this is the vendor's own case study.
  • Missoula, MT (remunicipalization, 2017). The city took Mountain Water from Carlyle-owned ownership by condemnation, paying about $88.6 million [15].
  • Prince George’s County, MD (stormwater CBP3). A 30-year partnership with Corvias to retrofit 2,000 impervious acres with green infrastructure. The initial phase was estimated at $100 million, with room to grow toward 15,000 acres [21].

3. Federal tools, funding and state enabling laws

  • WIFIA was created in 2014 (WRRDA, P.L. 113-121). Projects generally must cost $20 million or more ($5 million for communities of 25,000 or fewer). Loans usually cover up to 49% of costs, and total federal help is capped at 80% except for some rural projects. Private borrowers need a public sponsor [2][3]. CBO says it is too early to tell whether WIFIA will draw significant private financing into water [1].
  • State Revolving Funds (SRFs) through BIL. $11.7 billion each to the Drinking Water and Clean Water SRFs, $15 billion for lead service lines, and $10 billion for emerging contaminants: $4 billion through the Drinking Water SRF, $1 billion through the Clean Water SRF and $5 billion in WIIN grants. All of it flows mainly to public utilities [4].
  • Superfund and brownfields. BIL added $3.5 billion for Superfund cleanups and $1.5 billion for brownfields [4]. EPA's Superfund Redevelopment program works with local governments and private developers to put cleaned-up sites back into use [22].
  • State enabling laws. P3 authority for water varies by state. New Jersey has the 1995 Water Supply and Wastewater Public-Private Contracting Acts and the 2015 Water Infrastructure Protection Act, which lets distressed systems be sold or leased and can trigger a referendum [16]. The Water Collaborative Delivery Association, an industry group, tracks authority state by state [17].
This report did not verify current WIFIA loan totals or post-2025 changes in federal appropriations or policy, including any SRF cuts or rescissions. Check those before relying on these figures.

4. Environmental cleanup and restoration PPPs

  • Mitigation banking and in-lieu fee programs. The 2008 Corps/EPA Compensatory Mitigation Rule ranks mitigation bank credits first, then in-lieu fee programs, then permittee-responsible mitigation [18]. The ranking created a private market in which banks restore wetlands and streams ahead of time and sell credits to developers and public agencies.
  • Water quality trading. EPA's 2003 policy supports trading of nitrogen, phosphorus and sediment credits to meet TMDLs (pollution limits for a water body) at lower cost [19]. Virginia's Chesapeake Bay Nutrient Credit Exchange is a long-running example [20].
  • Stormwater and MS4 compliance through performance contracts. DOTs in Pennsylvania, Virginia and Maryland have used turnkey "full-delivery" contracts paid per pound of TMDL reduction or per acre of wetland credit [26]. Prince George's County uses a CBP3 for the same purpose [21].
  • Superfund and brownfield redevelopment pairs public cleanup money with private reuse investment [4][22].

5. RES (Resource Environmental Solutions)

"RES" here means Resource Environmental Solutions, LLC of Bellaire, Texas. A different company, RES (Renewable Energy Systems), develops renewable energy, so check which one you meant.
  • Ownership. RES was founded by Elliott Bouillion and calls itself the nation's largest ecological restoration company. In 2022 it took a major investment from Onex Partners V alongside existing investor KKR [23]. Onex lists it as a portfolio company since March 2022 [24].
  • Business model. RES offers environmental mitigation, stormwater, water quality, and climate and flood resilience. It handles full delivery, long-term stewardship and "guaranteed performance" [23]. In 2023 it said it operates more than 400 mitigation sites, and that its joint venture with Resource Land Holdings had acquired 24 conservation and mitigation banks, including Florida's 2,695-acre Bluefield Ranch [25].
  • Public-agency P3 work.
    • A PennDOT contract guaranteeing more than 573,000 lb of sediment reduction.
    • A Maryland State Highway Administration package of 22,592 linear feet of stream restoration across six sites, cutting 7,798 lb of nitrogen a year.
    • A VDOT project on Wancopin Creek combined with a privately funded mitigation bank.
    These figures come from a conference abstract by an RES staff member [26]. RES also won Louisiana coastal restoration projects from the state's Coastal Protection and Restoration Authority (CPRA) in 2015 [27].
Weakly sourced: the RES project numbers and cost-savings claims come from company press releases and an RES-authored abstract [23][25][26][27]. No independent audit was found. RES's revenue isn't public, and no newer ownership change after 2022 was confirmed.

6. Risks and criticisms

  • Rate increases. Revenue guarantees and equity returns pass straight to ratepayers. In Bayonne, rates rose more than 50% by 2019, and Rialto's equity return requirement was 19.6% pre-tax [7][10].
  • Contract design and lock-in. Contracts lasting 30 to 40 years are hard to foresee or renegotiate [1][5]. Atlanta's contract ended because both sides agreed it did not provide "an economically viable framework" [9].
  • Public health and accountability. See Pittsburgh's corrosion-control dispute and the Flint settlement [12][13].
  • Transparency and competition. Bayonne's procurement ended up with a single bidder after eight firms expressed interest [5].
  • Remunicipalization is possible but expensive, as Missoula shows [15].
  • Restoration markets. Credits depend on regulators' crediting protocols, such as the 50% default efficiency rate RES reported beating in Virginia, so whether gains are real depends on monitoring [26][19].

7. Where biochar and activated carbon fit

The companion biochar and activated carbon report found that granular activated carbon (GAC) is the proven Best Available Technology for PFAS, while biochar is cheaper and better suited to metals, nutrients and stormwater filters. That suggests two fits:

  • GAC for PFAS treatment paid for by BIL's emerging-contaminant SRF and WIIN funds [4]. These could be delivered as design-build or DBO projects with performance guarantees on media replacement.
  • Biochar-amended green infrastructure, such as bioretention and filter berms, inside pay-for-performance stormwater and nutrient contracts like CBP3 or DOT full-delivery deals [21][26].

This is inference. The report found no U.S. PPP that specifically uses biochar, and any nutrient credit would need an approved crediting protocol [19].

8. Key takeaways and open questions

  • U.S. water P3s are mostly about O&M and procurement. Public capital through SRFs, BIL and WIFIA is the main source of money [1][4].
  • Restoration is the fastest-growing private-delivery segment, and RES is its largest player [18][23].
  • To investigate next:
    • Current WIFIA and SRF funding levels after 2025.
    • Independent evaluations of the Prince George's CBP3.
    • RES's state DOT contract pipeline and any RES work on PFAS or biochar.
    • Bayonne's rates and renegotiation status after 2019.
    • Which states' P3 laws cover stormwater and nutrient pay-for-performance contracts [17].
