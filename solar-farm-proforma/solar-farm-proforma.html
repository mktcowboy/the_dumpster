Utility-Scale Solar · Wichita Falls, Texas · ERCOT Grid (Oncor)

The economics of a solar farm near Wichita Falls, on 1,000 acres

A utility-scale solar farm is a simple business with four moving parts: what it costs to build, how much sunlight the site converts to electricity, the contract price for that electricity, and a federal tax credit that currently pays for roughly a third of the plant. This page works through each part with current (October 2026) market figures, then builds a full 30-year proforma for a 1,000-acre greenfield project near Wichita Falls, Texas — about 150 MW of panels in ERCOT, roughly $170 million all-in — with every assumption adjustable. Texas changes three things versus the plains further north: the sun is a little better, the grid connects faster and cheaper, and property tax — not land — is the local cost that can break the deal.

Basis: hypothetical greenfield near Wichita Falls (Wichita / Archer County area), single-axis tracking, leased land, 30-year life, ERCOT North Zone, Oncor as transmission provider. All figures nominal USD. Researched October 12, 2026; sources linked throughout.

01 — The short version

How the money works

A solar farm has no fuel cost and almost no moving parts, so its economics are decided almost entirely on day one: the construction price, the power contract, and the tax credit. Build costs for a tracking plant run about $1.06 per watt in late 2026, before grid interconnection. The plant then sells its output under a power purchase agreement (PPA) — typically 10–25 years in ERCOT; the local precedent, TotalEnergies' 1 GW deal with Google covering its Wichita County project, is 15 years. Continental PPA prices averaged $61.40/MWh in Q2 2026, but ERCOT is the cheapest ISO in North America on LevelTen's index: its P25 (competitive-quartile) solar offer price was $49/MWh in Q4 2025, up from $44 in Q1 2025. That discount is the whole ballgame: Wichita Falls produces more energy per panel than central Oklahoma, but it sells that energy into the country's most competitive power market.

Two structural features carry the deal, and one Texas feature threatens it. First, the federal investment tax credit (ITC) rebates 30% of the build cost — worth roughly $51 million on this project — and it can be sold for cash at about 90–95¢ on the dollar. Second, ERCOT's “connect and manage” interconnection is far cheaper and faster than in SPP or MISO (Section 2). Against that, Texas property tax is a top cost driver: unabated, a plant this size could face roughly $14,300 per MW per year — over $2 million a year — so the Wichita County Chapter 312 abatement / PILOT negotiation in Section 5 is not paperwork, it is the margin. Land itself is still cheap: leasing 1,000 Texas acres at ~$850/acre/year costs about 6% of year-1 revenue. The timing catch also remains: under the 2025 OBBBA law, a project starting now keeps the 30% credit only if it is placed in service by December 31, 2027 — ERCOT's ~3-year path to operation is faster than SPP's, but it still does not beat that clock for a brand-new request. Section 6 takes that up directly.

02 — Build cost

What it costs to build

US utility-scale pricing in Q2 2026 put a single-axis tracking plant at ~$1.06M per MWdc ($1.06/W) and fixed-tilt at ~$0.95/W. Prices rose year over year — tracking +2.0% — on steel, aluminum, and copper tariffs and transport costs. Modules alone are about $0.33/W, with developers paying a premium for US-made modules to qualify for domestic-content credit adders. Trackers cost $0.15–0.35/W more than fixed-tilt but harvest roughly 4 points more capacity factor, which is why nearly every new plains-state plant tracks.

Source: Wood Mackenzie / SEIA US market pricing via pv magazine Global, Sep 25, 2026; tracker premium: National Laboratory of the Rockies data via pv-tech, Sep 2026.

Interconnection in ERCOT: the Texas advantage

ERCOT runs “connect and manage”: a generator does not fund network upgrades the way it does in SPP or MISO — congestion is managed instead through redispatch and curtailment, and power is delivered on an as-available basis. Oncor, the transmission provider for the Wichita Falls area, still has to study and build the physical connection (gen-tie, substation work, metering), but there is no $200+/kW network-upgrade bill. No ERCOT-specific $/kW interconnection cost is published Not published — the model below therefore uses a $75/kW allowance (~$11 million) for the connection itself, explicitly as a modeling allowance and not a benchmark, and lets you stress it to $225/kW if a screening study finds constrained capacity at the nearest substation. ERCOT also moves faster: studies can take ~1 year and a realistic request-to-operation path is ~3 years, versus a 5-plus-year national median — and ERCOT executed more interconnection agreements in 2024 than any other ISO.

Sources: Berkeley Lab “Queued Up” via pv magazine USA, Jan 7, 2026; timeline: Renewable Energy World, Sep 2026. The trade-off of connect-and-manage is curtailment risk, which this simplified model does not deduct — see Section 8.

03 — Land & sunlight

What 1,000 acres produces

Land → capacity

Utility solar needs 5–8 acres per MWdc all-in, including setbacks, access roads, and inverter pads; tracking plants sit at the denser end (~4–6). So 1,000 acres supports ~125–200 MWdc, with ~150 MWdc a reasonable midpoint. The model uses 6.7 acres/MWdc (≈149 MWdc).

A real-world check on density, in the same county: TotalEnergies' Wichita project — 805 MWp in total, with phase 1 at 625 MW on ~6,000 acres (about 9.6 acres/MW of total site), 1.56 million panels, ~$830 million of capex, construction from Q1 2026 toward a Q4 2027 operation date. Total-site acreage runs well above the fenced array footprint, which is why the model keeps land intensity adjustable.

Sources: Beacon Journal, Sep 8, 2025 (TotalEnergies Wichita County project); NREL array-density data.

Sunlight → energy

Wichita Falls gets 1,813 kWh/m²/yr of global horizontal irradiation — about 4.97 peak sun-hours a day — with unusually high direct irradiation (2,034 kWh/m²/yr) that favors trackers. That is ~4% more sun than Oklahoma City on the same dataset. A fixed-tilt plant at the optimum 32° tilt yields 1,655 kWh per kWdc there; a tracking plant is estimated at ~1,900 kWh per kWdc in year 1 — a ~21.7% DC capacity factor, roughly 27–28% on the AC side at the typical 1.3 inverter loading ratio. Output then degrades 0.45–0.55% per year.

Tracking figure is an estimate The Global Solar Atlas calculator has no tracking configuration; the ~1,900 figure adds the tracker gain Berkeley Lab documents (~+3–5 points of AC capacity factor) to the Atlas fixed-tilt yield for this exact site. Treat the exact yield as an open question until a site-specific production study (P50/P90) exists.

Sources: Global Solar Atlas v2.13 (World Bank / ESMAP / Solargis), Wichita Falls site, read Oct 12, 2026; Oklahoma City comparison from the same Atlas; Berkeley Lab, Utility-Scale Solar 2024.

04 — Revenue

The power contract is the business

Almost no utility solar is built on speculation. The plant signs a PPA — a utility or corporate buyer commits to 10–25 years of output at a fixed price, often with an escalator — and that contract is what lenders lend against. ERCOT is where the country's data-center buyers are shopping (Google's 1 GW, 15-year Texas deal is the local example), but it is also the cheapest power market on the continent for a seller:

No ERCOT-specific P25 for Q1–Q2 2026 was published.

The ERCOT discount — now with a number on it

Unlike SPP, ERCOT has a published regional price: $49/MWh P25 in Q4 2025, rising fast ($32 in early 2022, $44 in early 2025) on data-center demand, tariffs, and the tax-credit deadline — but still ~$13/MWh below the continental average. The model defaults to $52/MWh, just under the Q2 2026 continental P25, on the view that a 2026-vintage ERCOT deal with a creditworthy buyer prices at or modestly above the last published ERCOT P25. Base case is a judgment The sensitivity table in Section 7 shows what $44 and $49 do to returns — use those rows if you want the strictly-sourced ERCOT prices.

Why you can't just sell into the market

ERCOT wholesale energy is below PPA levels and violently variable: the market monitor put the 2024 real-time average at $31.91/MWh (all-in $34), with 2025 indicators near ~$30 — and solar's capture price, what a solar plant actually realizes, swung from ~$25/MWh in the North zone on a mild December 2025 day to ~$69/MWh during a cold snap in Platts' daily index. Midday solar cannibalization is the structural story in ERCOT; an annual capture rate could not be verified Not published. Wholesale energy alone does not carry a plant — the PPA does — which is why the model's post-contract merchant tail (years 26–30) is priced at $30/MWh and contributes so little.

Sources: Potomac Economics, ERCOT 2024 State of the Market (PDF); Platts capture-price commentaries via TradingView, Dec 3 & Dec 15, 2025.

05 — Running costs & land

Cheap to run, not free

Operating costs

  • O&M: ~$10–20 per kWdc-year all-in for a 2026-vintage plant (NREL's modeled benchmark is $20; IRENA's 2024 fleet average $13.1; Berkeley Lab's older pure-O&M survey $5–8). The model uses $15.
  • Insurance — the hail problem: Wichita Falls sits in hail alley. Texas and Southeast solar premiums rose as much as 50% in a single year in BloombergNEF's survey, coverage is capped in high-risk areas, and insurance can exceed 30% of a Texas project's operating costs. Hail-stow trackers materially cut premiums (one Texas project cut insurance ~72% with hail measures). Base insurance range $5–12 per kWac-year nationally; asset management another $3–6. Model: $18 combined, above the national midpoint, for the hail loading.
  • Inverters: budget a $15–30/kWac replacement around years 12–15. Model: year 13 at $22/kWac (~$2.5M).
  • Degradation: 0.45–0.55%/yr after a ~1.5–2.5% first-year step (fleet data from Berkeley Lab suggests up to 0.9%/yr system-level on newer plants — the slider goes there).

Sources: NREL/DOE benchmarks; IRENA 2024; Berkeley Lab 2024; hail/insurance: BloombergNEF via EnergyConnects, Aug 2023 and pv magazine, May 12, 2026. Inverter, insurance, and asset-management ranges rest partly on a 2026 industry proforma reference and are single-source.

Land: lease, don't buy

The dominant model is a 30-year lease (plus extensions) at ~$800/acre/year typical in Texas, with West Texas offers of $750–1,000 and a North Texas midpoint around $800–900 — about $850,000/yr for this project at the model's $850 default, and a life-changing annuity for the landowner relative to farm income. Buying instead would cost ~$3.1M up front at the USDA's 2026 Texas farm real-estate average of $3,100/acre (statewide rural-land surveys run higher, ~$5,218/acre in Q2 2026, skewed by metro-fringe sales; no Wichita County-specific price was found Not published), but most developers lease to keep capital in the plant — TotalEnergies' Wichita County land is leased.

Sources: Texas developer OCI Energy via Commercial Observer, May 2024; USDA NASS Land Values 2026 via Texas A&M AgriLife; Texas Real Estate Research Center statewide figures via thetexaslandagent.com.

Texas property tax: negotiate the PILOT or pay full freight

Texas has no state income tax, so counties appraise a solar plant at market value and property tax becomes a top operating cost. There is no school-district relief for solar: the Chapter 403 “JETI Act” (successor to the expired Chapter 313) explicitly excludes solar, wind, and storage. What remains is a Chapter 312 county abatement — up to 100% for up to 10 years. Wichita County's own precedent, set for the TotalEnergies project in fall 2025, is a 10-year PILOT of $1,253,648/yr on a ≥$830M / 625 MW project (≈$2,006/MW/yr) — about $300,000/yr scaled to this 149 MWdc project. Without any abatement, the one Texas project with a published figure (350 MW in Concho County) expects ~$5M/yr — ≈$14,300/MW/yr Single-source proxy — over $2.1M/yr here. The model therefore runs the PILOT rate for years 1–10 and the full rate from year 11, with both as sliders: if the county says no, set the PILOT to the full rate and watch the tail. One legislative risk: SB 819 (2025) would have banned abatements for ≥10 MW renewables; it died in the House in May 2025 but could return in the 2027 session.

Sources: Wichita County PILOT via Citizen Portal, Nov 2025 and reinvestment zone, Oct 2025; Chapter 403 exclusion: KE Andrews, May 2026; unabated proxy: Construction Review Online, Jan 2026; SB 819: Electrek, May 28, 2025.

Decommissioning is in the lease — by statute

Texas Utilities Code Chapter 302, as amended by HB 3228 (effective September 1, 2025), requires every solar lease signed on or after that date to provide for removal of the facilities and restoration of the land to near-original condition, backed by financial assurance — parent guaranty, letter of credit, or bond — covering removal, recycling/disposal, and restoration net of salvage. The cost estimate must be updated every five years, clauses waiving the obligation are void, and HB 3228 adds a duty to recycle all practicably recyclable components, including the modules themselves. Wichita County's TotalEnergies PILOT likewise includes a third-party-held decommissioning bond. Enacted — unlike many states, this is settled law, not a proposal.

Sources: Texas Utilities Code Ch. 302; Foley & Lardner summary via Mondaq, May 2025; pv magazine USA, May 27, 2025.

06 — Tax credits, financing & the grid queue

Where the deal is really made

⚠ The December 31, 2027 cliff

The ITC is 30% of build cost (with prevailing-wage and apprenticeship compliance) — about $51 million on this project. But under the OBBBA law, a project keeps that 30% only if it began construction by July 4, 2026 (already past) or is placed in service by December 31, 2027. A greenfield project starting today therefore has ~14 months to be fully built and energized — against an ERCOT path that, while the fastest in the country, still runs ~3 years from request to operation. Even Texas speed does not beat this clock for a brand-new request. In practice, a new Wichita Falls greenfield must either acquire a project that already holds interconnection position and safe-harbored status, or underwrite the deal with a much smaller (or zero) credit. Note the local precedent is racing the same clock: TotalEnergies' phase 1 targets Q4 2027 operation — just inside the deadline. The model's ITC slider goes to 0% so you can see what that world looks like: at $52/MWh, it is not pretty.

Sources: SEIA tax policy page, updated Oct 2026; Enphase and industry guidance on OBBBA deadlines, 2026. Adders exist beyond 30% — +10 points each for domestic content and energy-community siting — but none are in the base case.

The credit, and the alternative

The 30% ITC (§48E) can be sold for cash under credit transferability at roughly 90–95¢ per dollar of credit — the model assumes a sale at 93¢ in year 1, which is how many sponsors monetize it without a tax-equity partner. The alternative production tax credit (§45Y) pays $31/MWh for 10 years in 2026; Berkeley Lab found the PTC beat the ITC for 54 of 75 recent projects, and a high-yield Wichita Falls tracker is exactly the profile where it can win — but it faces the same OBBBA deadlines and needs project-specific modeling, so the proforma uses the ITC. Depreciation adds a second, quieter benefit: solar is 5-year MACRS property and OBBBA permanently restored 100% bonus depreciation for property acquired after January 19, 2025.

Sources: IRS 2026 PTC adjustment (Novogradac); SEIA MACRS guidance; Berkeley Lab 2024.

How it's financed

  • Debt cost: SOFR + 175–275 bps → ~5.75–7.0% all-in. Model: 6.25%.
  • Structure: total leverage ~70–75% of capital when tax equity is counted; senior debt sized to a DSCR ≥ 1.30× on P50 production, tenor ~18 years.
  • Tax equity (partnership flips) targets 6–8% yields and takes the credits plus depreciation.
  • Sponsor targets: levered IRRs of 12–18% are quoted in industry guides, with recent core-project returns nearer 8–11%. Directional Treat these as aspirations, not observations — the model below shows what the sourced inputs actually produce.

Source: 2025–26 project-finance market references; corroborated against a generic 2025 project-finance model. Rate and structure figures are single-source in the underlying research.

The queue is shorter in Texas — the crowd is not

Nationally, the median project reaching operation in 2025 waited more than five years from interconnection request to commercial operation. ERCOT is the outlier: studies can complete in ~1 year and a realistic request-to-operation path is ~3 years, because connect-and-manage skips the network-upgrade studies that stall other grids. The catch is volume and attrition: ERCOT's June 2026 queue held ~434 GW of projects, ~152 GW of it solar Single-source tally, and historically only ~29% of ERCOT queue entrants have ever reached operation (solar ~22%). ERCOT did synchronize ~23 GW in 2024–25, so the machine works — but a queue position near Wichita Falls still competes with every data-center-driven project in North Texas, and no Oncor substation-level capacity figure is public: that takes a transmission screening study.

Sources: Berkeley Lab “Queued Up” via pv magazine USA, Jan 7, 2026; Renewable Energy World, Sep 2026; queue tally: ERCOT GIS analysis, Jun 2026 (single source).

07 — The proforma

1,000 acres, built out: the 30-year model

The base case below is assembled only from the figures in Sections 2–6: 1,000 acres near Wichita Falls at 6.7 acres/MWdc (≈149 MWdc, ~115 MWac), tracking EPC at $1.06/W plus a $75/kW ERCOT interconnection allowance, a $52/MWh PPA with a 2% escalator for 25 years (the model keeps a 25-year term for comparability; ERCOT deals are often shorter — the local Google deal is 15 years), and the 30% ITC sold at 93¢. Every input is a slider — stress the PPA down to the sourced ERCOT P25 of $49 (or $44), the PILOT to zero relief, or the ITC to zero and watch the returns. Fixed assumptions: 1.30 inverter loading ratio; O&M and other opex escalating 2.5%/yr; land lease escalating 2%/yr; inverter replacement in year 13; 18-year level-payment debt; property tax at the Wichita County PILOT rate in years 1–10 and the full unabated rate from year 11; pre-tax project cash flows; no curtailment deduction and no terminal salvage value. One consequence to notice in the table: at a $30/MWh merchant price under full property tax, years 26–30 run at negative EBITDA — the model operates the plant anyway, but a real sponsor would recontract, repower, or retire at PPA expiry rather than burn cash.

08 — Open questions

What the research could not verify

  • ERCOT average PPA price. LevelTen publishes the ERCOT P25 ($49/MWh, Q4 2025) but not the ERCOT mean, and no ERCOT-specific P25 for Q1–Q2 2026 was published. The model's $52/MWh base case is a judgment anchored to those two points — the single most important number in the model.
  • Property tax after the PILOT. The $14,300/MW/yr unabated figure is a single-source proxy from a Concho County project, not a Wichita County appraisal. The actual post-PILOT bill depends on the appraisal district and any renewal — get the county's figure before trusting years 11–30.
  • ERCOT interconnection cost. No published $/kW figure exists for ERCOT connect-and-manage; the $75/kW in the model is an allowance for the physical connection, not a benchmark, and Oncor substation-level capacity near Wichita Falls requires a screening study.
  • Solar capture price & curtailment. Only daily Platts snapshots were found (~$25–69/MWh); no annual ERCOT solar capture rate, negative-price share, or curtailment rate could be verified. The model deducts none of these, which flatters merchant exposure.
  • Site yield. The ~1,900 kWh/kWdc tracking yield is estimated from the Global Solar Atlas fixed-tilt yield (1,655) plus documented tracker gain, not a site production study (P50/P90 engineering report).
  • Wichita County land values. No county-specific purchase price or lease rate was found; the page uses Texas / North Texas figures (~$800–900/acre lease; $3,100/acre USDA farm real estate).
  • Component-level build cost. Only the module price ($0.33/W) and tracker premium ($0.15–0.35/W) are current and verified; the rest of the $1.06/W stack is not published at usable granularity for 2025–26.
  • Return benchmarks. Quoted sponsor IRR targets (12–18%) and financing spreads rest partly on single or weak sources; the model's outputs — computed from the harder numbers above — land below those targets at ERCOT-realistic prices, which is itself the finding.
