00 / Executive summary

The short version

One year after the late-2025 peak, crypto is cheaper but not smaller. The interesting rotation is inside the market: out of beta, into dollars, credit, and cash-flow infrastructure.

The headline
Two markets
BTC is down 33% from its high and ETH is down 49%, yet total market cap is still __TOTAL_MCAP__ and stablecoin supply is __STABLE_TOTAL__. Speculative beta is compressed; the dollar layer is compounding.
The rotation
Beta → carry
Capital is not leaving the industry. It is moving into T-bill-backed stablecoins, tokenized credit, basis trades, and yield-bearing dollars. __STABLE_COUNT__ stablecoins and tokenized funds now sit in the top 100.

The first thing to understand about this snapshot is that the bear market is real but narrow. Bitcoin trades near __BTC_PRICE__, roughly one-third below its October 2025 high. Ethereum trades near __ETH_PRICE__, roughly half its August 2025 high. The total market is __TOTAL_MCAP__, and the top 10 assets still account for __TOP10_SHARE__ of it. That is not a market that has been abandoned; it is a market that has been re-priced and concentrated.

The second thing is that the growth is in dollars. Stablecoin supply is __STABLE_TOTAL__, about __STABLE_SHARE__ of total crypto market cap. Sixteen stablecoins plus five tokenized T-bill or credit funds sit in the top 100. Figure HELOC, a tokenized home-equity line, is a top-10 asset by reported market cap. The industry's biggest product is no longer a token that goes up; it is a dollar that moves.

The third thing is that leadership is narrow and often low-float. Privacy assets (ZEC, XMR) and perp DEX tokens (HYPE, ASTER, LIGHTER) are among the strongest narratives. New listings such as Bitway (BTW) can post enormous percentage gains while a small share of supply circulates. The FDV / market cap column in the top-100 table is there for a reason: in this tape, float matters as much as the story.

The operator's edge in this market is not picking the next 100x. It is measurement: knowing where liquidity actually is, which yields are real, which wallets are worth following, which traders have an edge versus leverage, and which stablecoins are worth holding. The rest of this page is organized around those questions.

The float problem

In a market where broad beta is scarce, low-float tokens become the trade. A small circulating supply makes price easy to move, especially when a new listing has a compelling narrative and market makers are thin. The catch is that the fully diluted valuation can be many times the market cap — and the unlocks eventually arrive. This is not a reason to avoid every low-float asset, but it is a reason to know what you are buying.

How to read the table

Market cap is price × circulating supply. FDV is price × fully diluted supply. Vol / MC above roughly 10% is high turnover; below 1% is thin. A stablecoin’s market cap is supply, not demand — do not compare it to an equity-like token without adjusting your question.

The narrative trap

A sector can be structurally important and still be a bad trade. L2s are essential infrastructure and poor tokens. Stablecoins are the most important product in crypto and mostly not investable as equity. Separate “is this real?” from “does this token capture value?”

04 / Market structure

Where the liquidity actually lives

Centralized exchanges still dominate price discovery. On-chain, the flow splits between Ethereum settlement, Solana and Base retail venues, Tron dollar rails, and Hyperliquid perps.

Market structure is the part of crypto that does not fit in a price chart. The same asset can trade at slightly different prices on a centralized venue, an AMM, an aggregator, and a perp exchange. The venues are connected by arbitrage, bridges, market makers, and a small number of settlement layers. Understanding that plumbing explains most “weird” price action.

The liquidity stack

  1. Price discovery: centralized exchanges and large perp venues set the reference price. On-chain pools follow via arbitrage.
  2. Execution: retail flow hits aggregators, wallets, and app-specific interfaces. Those route into AMMs, RFQ desks, or centralized books.
  3. Settlement: stablecoins and major assets move across chains through canonical bridges, third-party bridges, or exchange withdrawals.
  4. Leverage: perp DEXs and centralized perps add leverage on top of spot. Funding and liquidations transmit stress back to spot.
  5. Accounting: TVL, volume, and reserves are all measured differently. Most “market share” claims depend on which layer is being counted.
Read the venue, not the headline

A dollar of DEX volume is not a dollar of revenue. A dollar of TVL is not a dollar of deposits. A dollar of exchange reserves is not a dollar of customer funds. The most common analytical error in crypto is comparing metrics that share a unit but not a meaning.

Bitcoin: the reserve asset

Bitcoin’s 2024 halving cut issuance to roughly 3.125 BTC per block, and the next halving is due in 2028. The supply schedule is predictable; demand is not. The marginal buyer has shifted from retail to ETFs, corporate treasuries, and collateral desks. That makes BTC more liquid and more correlated to macro liquidity, but it also means the drawdowns can be slower and more orderly than the retail-driven cycles of the past.

The Bitcoin ecosystem is broadening beyond the asset itself: Stacks and other L2s, Bitway’s Bitcoin-compatible chain and yield products, Babylon restaking, and the long tail of Ordinals and Runes. Most of these are small relative to BTC’s market cap. The important point is that BTC is no longer only a buy-and-hold asset; it is becoming collateral with a yield curve.

Ethereum: the collateral layer

Ethereum still settles the largest share of on-chain value, and its DeFi stack remains the deepest: Aave V3 alone holds about __AAVE_TVL__, Morpho Blue about __MORPHO_TVL__, and EigenCloud about __EIGEN_TVL__ in restaking. Staking yields are modest — roughly __ETH_STAKING_APY__ for liquid staking — but they are real, and they anchor a large collateral market.

The bear case is structural: L2s captured activity and fees while ETH’s issuance and burn mechanism did not produce a strong enough supply sink. The bull case is that Ethereum remains the settlement layer for tokenized assets and stablecoins, and that a recovery in on-chain credit will bring demand for ETH collateral. At this snapshot, ETH is down nearly half from its high and underperforming BTC over 30 days. The market is still waiting for the bull case to show up in the token price.

The ETH/BTC question

ETH/BTC is at the __ETH_BTC_PCT__ percentile of its one-year range and __ETH_BTC_30D__ over 30 days. A mean-reversion trade is not a thesis. Before positioning for ETH outperformance, identify the catalyst that brings incremental demand to ETH rather than to L2s, staking wrappers, or stablecoins.

How a stablecoin breaks

1. Reserve shock

A bank failure, a duration mismatch, or a frozen account makes the issuer’s assets worth less than the liabilities. USDC’s 2023 depeg during the SVB weekend is the canonical example: the peg recovered, but only after the issuer disclosed its exposure and the backstop arrived.

2. Collateral cascade

Crypto-backed stablecoins depend on liquidators showing up. In a fast market, collateral gaps, oracle delays, and bridge congestion can push the stablecoin below peg before governance can react.

3. Funding inversion

Basis-trade stablecoins earn when funding is positive. When funding flips negative for long enough, the yield becomes a cost and the stablecoin’s supply can shrink quickly.

4. Redemption gates

A stablecoin is only as good as its redemption. If the issuer can pause minting or redemption, or if the on-chain wrapper depends on a permissioned off-ramp, the “peg” is a promise rather than an arbitrage.

5. Bridge and wrapper risk

Most stablecoin supply on a given chain is not native issuance; it is a bridged or wrapped claim. The underlying issuer may be solid while the bridge is not. Always distinguish native USDC from bridged USDC.

6. Governance and upgrade risk

Upgradeable contracts and admin keys can change collateral rules, fees, or redemption terms. Read the governance docs, not just the audit badge.

Regulation in one screen

The regulatory direction is toward reserve requirements, disclosure, and perimeter rules. In the United States, the GENIUS Act created a federal framework for payment stablecoins, including reserve and disclosure expectations for permitted issuers. The EU’s MiCA regime imposes reserve, governance, and disclosure requirements on stablecoin issuers and has been fully applicable since late 2024. Singapore, the UAE, Hong Kong, Switzerland, and Japan each have their own licensing regimes. The practical takeaway for holders is simple: issuer jurisdiction and reserve composition matter, and the regulatory wrapper is now part of the credit analysis.

Stablecoin due diligence

  • What backs it? T-bills, bank deposits, commercial paper, crypto collateral, or a mix. Get the latest reserve report, not the marketing page.
  • Who audits it? An attestation is not a full audit. Check the auditor, the frequency, and whether the report covers all entities and chains.
  • Who can redeem? Direct redemption for institutions only? Minimum size? Gate fees? What happens on weekends?
  • Where is the native issuance? A bridged version inherits bridge risk. Prefer native issuance on the chain you use.
  • What is the yield source? If the stablecoin pays more than T-bills, the excess is compensation for credit, duration, funding, or smart contract risk.
  • What is the governance? Upgradeable contracts, admin keys, and multisig thresholds. Who can freeze or change rules?
  • What is the liquidity? Depth on the venues you use, not just aggregate supply. A $5B stablecoin can still have a thin market on your chain.
  • What is the tail risk? Bank exposure, commercial paper, counterparty concentration, and regulatory action in the issuer’s jurisdiction.
The stablecoin insight

Stablecoins are not a sideshow to the crypto market; they are the market’s cash account. The supply trend is one of the cleanest signals of whether capital is entering, leaving, or simply waiting. In this snapshot, supply is flat over 30 days while prices are down — capital is waiting, not leaving.

07 / Yield farming

Yield is compensation, not free money

Every sustainable yield comes from someone paying for liquidity, leverage, credit, or risk transfer. The question is not “what is the APY?” but “who is paying, and what happens when they stop?”

Yield farming is the practice of deploying capital into on-chain protocols to earn fees, interest, rewards, or points. The high-level mechanics are simple: you lend, provide liquidity, stake, or take the other side of a basis trade. The complexity is in the risk stack. A 20% APY can be a fair payment for providing liquidity in a volatile pair, or it can be a subsidy that disappears the moment the token emissions end.

The 12-point farm check

  • Is the base yield from real fees or interest, or is it mostly emissions?
  • What is the 30-day average APY versus the current APY?
  • How much TVL is in the pool, and how concentrated are LPs?
  • Is the pool stablecoin-only, or are you short volatility without knowing it?
  • What is the impermanent loss at −30%, −50%, and −70%?
  • Are the contracts audited, and who can upgrade them?
  • Is the oracle reliable, and what happens if it lags?
  • What is the withdrawal queue or lock-up?
  • Are rewards paid in a token you would hold, or must you sell them?
  • What is the gas and bridge cost to enter and exit?
  • What happens if the chain halts or the bridge is exploited?
  • Can you explain the strategy to someone else in two sentences?
Red flags

APY above 100% on a stablecoin pair; anonymous team with admin keys; TVL that doubled in a week; rewards paid in a token with thin liquidity; a “risk-free” loop; withdrawal fees that appear only when you try to exit; yield that depends on a points program with no published terms.

The yield insight

The best risk-adjusted yield in crypto is usually boring: T-bill-backed dollars, blue-chip lending, and staking. The highest APYs are compensation for risks that are hard to see until they materialize. Size the position so that a total loss is annoying, not fatal.

08 / Wallet tracking

Wallets are the atomic unit of on-chain truth

An address is a ledger entry. A labeled entity is intelligence. The difference is resolution: who owns it, what it holds, who it trades with, and what it does next.

Wallet tracking is the practice of turning raw addresses into a watchlist of entities whose behavior you understand. It is useful for four things: following smart money, monitoring protocol health, finding airdrops and early opportunities, and catching your own security mistakes. It is not useful as a copy-trading signal without context — a wallet can be a market maker, a bridge, a treasury, or a bot.

Five questions a wallet should answer

1 · Who
Entity, not address
Is this a fund, a market maker, a DAO treasury, a bridge, an exchange hot wallet, or a person? The label changes the interpretation.
2 · How much
Net worth & stable ratio
Total value, stablecoin share, and concentration. A whale with 90% in one token is a different risk than a diversified treasury.
3 · What
Positions & leverage
Lending, LP, perps, staking, and vaults. Leverage is often hidden across multiple protocols.
4 · With whom
Counterparties
Who sends funds in and out? Repeated counterparties reveal the real network behind an address.
5 · What next
Behavior change
New approvals, large withdrawals, collateral changes, and governance votes are the signals worth alerting on.
The rule
Track entities
A watchlist of 20 well-understood entities beats 2,000 random addresses. Context is the edge.

The workflow

  1. Define the question. “Is smart money accumulating ETH?” is trackable. “What is the market doing?” is not.
  2. Pick the chain and venue. Most flow is on Ethereum, Solana, Base, Arbitrum, BSC, Tron, and Hyperliquid. Start where the asset actually trades.
  3. Resolve the entity. Use labels from Arkham/Nansen, exchange deposit patterns, multisig signers, and protocol docs. Do not assume a label is correct because a dashboard says so.
  4. Build a small watchlist. 10–30 addresses with a clear reason for each. Record the thesis and the review date.
  5. Set alerts. Large transfers, new approvals, collateral changes, governance votes, and balance thresholds. Alerts should be specific enough to act on.
  6. Review and prune. Most wallets are noise after a few weeks. Keep the ones that taught you something.

Wallet security

  • Use a hardware wallet for meaningful balances.
  • Keep a separate hot wallet for experiments and a cold wallet for savings.
  • Never enter a seed phrase into a website, chat, or browser extension.
  • Verify the domain before signing; bookmark the apps you use.
  • Read the transaction simulation, not just the button label.
  • Revoke unused token approvals regularly.
  • Use a multisig for shared or protocol treasury funds.
  • Test with a small transaction before a large one.
  • Beware address poisoning and clipboard malware: verify the full address.
  • Assume any unsolicited token or NFT is a trap.
The tracking trap

On-chain data is public but not self-explanatory. A wallet labeled “smart money” may be a market maker hedging elsewhere, a bridge, or a hacked account. Copying a single visible trade without the full position, cost basis, and hedge is not tracking — it is guessing with extra steps.

09 / Trader tracking

Separate edge from leverage

A leaderboard shows outcomes, not process. The useful question is whether a trader’s returns come from repeatable skill, a single lucky trade, or hidden risk that has not blown up yet.

Trader tracking has three layers: performance, risk, and behavior. Performance is the easiest to measure and the least useful on its own. Risk explains how the returns were produced. Behavior tells you whether the trader is repeatable or just currently lucky. On-chain perp venues like Hyperliquid make all three more visible than centralized exchanges do, but they also make survivorship bias and wash trading easier to mistake for skill.

Copy trading: the fine print

What can go wrong

  • Latency. You see the trade after it has moved. The leader’s entry is not your entry.
  • Sizing. A 2% position for a $10M account is not 2% for a $10k account after fees and slippage.
  • Hidden hedges. The visible perp may be offset by spot, options, or another venue you cannot see.
  • Different mandates. A market maker’s inventory trade is not a directional signal.
  • Survivorship. The leaderboard shows the survivors, not the accounts that blew up last month.

How to evaluate

  • At least 6–12 months of history across different regimes.
  • Positive expectancy after fees and slippage, not just gross PnL.
  • Drawdowns you could actually sit through.
  • A strategy you can explain in plain language.
  • Position sizes that fit your capital and risk budget.

Evaluate a trader in 15 minutes

  1. Check the time window. 30 days is a vibe; 12 months is a sample. Ask for both.
  2. Normalize by capital. Compare ROI and drawdown, not raw PnL.
  3. Look for concentration. One asset, one venue, one trade, or one funding regime?
  4. Check the downside. What was the worst month, and how did they behave after it?
  5. Separate beta from alpha. In a market that fell 30%, a short book can look brilliant.
  6. Find the hidden leg. Spot, options, borrow, or another account that offsets the visible position.
  7. Compare to a benchmark. Beating a 60/40 crypto index is different from beating a directional short.
  8. Write the thesis. If you cannot explain why the edge exists, you are the exit liquidity.
The trader insight

Most active traders do not beat the market after costs. In this snapshot, only __HL_POSITIVE__ of the tracked active Hyperliquid cohort was profitable over 30 days, and the median 30-day ROI was __HL_MEDIAN__. That does not mean trading is impossible; it means the base rate is harsh and the leaderboard is a selection filter, not a random sample.

10 / Valuation

What is the token actually claiming?

Crypto valuation is not one model; it is a set of questions about cash flow, collateral demand, dilution, and reflexivity. Start with what the token owns, then decide which metric is honest.

Most crypto tokens are not equity. They may be gas, collateral, governance, a claim on fees, a claim on a treasury, or none of the above. Valuation starts by classifying the claim. A token with a fee switch and a buyback can be modeled like a cash-flow asset. A gas token with no fee capture is a monetary asset whose value depends on demand for blockspace. A governance token with no economic rights is an option on future governance decisions. These are different assets and should not be compared on the same multiple.

Value accrual mechanisms

Fee share
Cash to holders
The protocol routes a share of fees to stakers, LPs, or a treasury. The cleanest link between usage and token value — if the share is real and not governance-dependent.
Burn
Supply sink
Fees or gas are used to buy back and burn tokens. Works when usage is high and the burn is not offset by emissions.
Staking
Security demand
Validators must hold and lock the token. Creates structural demand, but also inflation and unbonding risk.
Collateral
Balance-sheet demand
The token is accepted as collateral in lending markets. Demand rises with credit creation, and falls with liquidation cascades.
Governance
Option on control
A claim on future decisions, not current cash flow. Value depends on the treasury and the credible threat of a fee switch.
Monetary premium
Store of value
Demand for a scarce, liquid, censorship-resistant asset. Hard to model, easy to observe in dominance and ETF flows.

Three questions before any token

  1. What does the token claim on? Fees, gas, collateral, governance, treasury, or nothing. If the answer is “the ecosystem,” keep asking.
  2. Who pays, and why now? Identify the source of demand: users, borrowers, traders, validators, or a subsidy. A token that only appreciates because new buyers arrive is a reflexivity trade.
  3. What is the dilution path? Circulating supply, unlock schedule, emissions, and treasury sales. A great protocol with a 10× FDV overhang can be a bad token.
The valuation trap

In a risk-on market, narratives and float dominate. In a risk-off market, cash flow and buybacks matter more. The same token can look cheap on P/F at the top and expensive on P/F at the bottom because fees collapse faster than price. Always stress-test the denominator.

Token checklist

  • Can I explain the value accrual in one sentence without using the word “ecosystem”?
  • Is the fee/revenue figure gross or net of incentives?
  • What percentage of supply is circulating, and when are the next unlocks?
  • Who holds the treasury, and what can they do with it?
  • Is the token needed to use the protocol, or is it optional governance?
  • What happens to the token if usage doubles but fees stay flat?
  • What is the bear case, and is it already priced in?

Tax: the boring part that compounds

  • Record every trade, transfer, reward, and fee in a consistent ledger.
  • Track cost basis by lot; methods differ by jurisdiction.
  • Staking, lending, and liquidity rewards are usually income when received.
  • LP deposits and withdrawals can be taxable events; get advice.
  • Airdrops and forks may be income at receipt or at sale.
  • Cross-chain bridges and wraps need a clear accounting policy.
  • US brokers are phasing in 1099-DA reporting; reconcile it with your own records.
  • Wash-sale and loss rules vary; do not assume TradFi treatment.
  • Keep wallet-level records, not just exchange statements.
  • If you cannot explain a transaction to an auditor, document it now.

The security rule

There is no yield high enough to justify a seed phrase. There is no support agent who needs your screen. There is no airdrop that requires your private key. Most losses are not clever exploits; they are a signature you should not have given.

12 / Playbooks

Five ways to use this page

Turn the report into a repeatable process: a market dashboard, a stablecoin policy, a farm checklist, a wallet network, and a trader evaluation.

Playbook A

Build a weekend market dashboard

  1. Pick five metrics: total market cap, BTC dominance, stablecoin supply, DEX volume, and perp open interest.
  2. Pull them from CoinGecko and DefiLlama into one sheet or script. Store the date and source for every row.
  3. Add a 30-day and 90-day change column. Absolute levels are less useful than direction.
  4. Write one sentence of interpretation per metric. If you cannot, drop the metric.
  5. Review weekly, not hourly. The point is regime awareness, not dopamine.
Playbook B

Write a stablecoin treasury policy

  1. Set the liquidity bucket: 1–3 months of expenses in same-day redeemable dollars.
  2. Diversify issuers and chains; avoid a single bank, issuer, or bridge.
  3. Separate operating cash, reserve cash, and yield-seeking cash.
  4. Define eligible collateral and a maximum yield-product allocation.
  5. Document redemption procedures, signers, and a quarterly review.
Playbook C

Run a farm through 12 questions

  1. Identify the source of yield: fees, interest, emissions, points, or leverage.
  2. Model the APY at 0%, 50%, and 100% of current rewards.
  3. Compute IL at −30%, −50%, and −70% for volatile pools.
  4. Check TVL concentration, audit status, admin keys, and withdrawal terms.
  5. Size so a total loss is annoying, not existential. Write the exit plan before entering.
Playbook D

Build a 20-wallet intelligence network

  1. Choose 20 entities across categories: funds, market makers, protocols, whales, and bridges.
  2. Label each one with evidence, not vibes. Record the source and date.
  3. Set alerts for large transfers, approvals, and collateral changes.
  4. Review weekly: what changed, and does it confirm or contradict your thesis?
  5. Prune aggressively. A noisy watchlist is worse than no watchlist.
Playbook E

Evaluate a trader in 15 minutes

  1. Get 12 months of history, not 30 days.
  2. Compute expectancy after fees, profit factor, and max drawdown.
  3. Normalize by capital and compare to a simple benchmark.
  4. Find the hidden leg: spot, options, borrow, or another venue.
  5. Write the edge in one sentence. If you cannot, do not copy.
Playbook F

Build a sector basket

  1. Pick a theme with a measurable driver: stablecoin supply, perp volume, RWA TVL, or AI compute demand.
  2. Choose 5–8 assets with different value-capture mechanisms, not five copies of the same beta.
  3. Cap position sizes and set a rebalance rule. Equal weight is a feature, not a bug.
  4. Track the driver, not just the price. If the driver stalls, the basket should change.
  5. Write the exit conditions before you enter.

13 / Glossary

The words that matter

A working vocabulary for the rest of the page and for reading crypto research without getting lost in jargon.

AMM
Automated market maker. A smart contract that quotes prices using a formula and pooled liquidity instead of an order book.
APY
Annual percentage yield. A compounded annual rate; compare the compounding assumptions before comparing products.
Basis trade
Long spot and short a futures or perpetual contract to earn the spread or funding. Market-neutral until funding flips or a venue fails.
Bid-ask spread
The difference between the best buy and sell price. A measure of immediate liquidity cost.
Bridge
A protocol that moves assets or messages between chains. Bridge TVL is a claim on locked collateral, not a bank deposit.
CEX / DEX
Centralized exchange (custodial order book) versus decentralized exchange (on-chain liquidity). The line is blurring as CEXs add on-chain settlement.
Collateral factor
The share of an asset’s value that can be borrowed against in a lending market. Higher factors mean more leverage and more liquidation risk.
Concentrated liquidity
An AMM design where LPs choose price ranges. Higher capital efficiency, higher impermanent loss and management complexity.
Depeg
A stablecoin trading away from its target. A small, brief deviation is normal; a persistent one is a credit event.
DePIN
Decentralized physical infrastructure network. Tokens that coordinate hardware such as storage, compute, wireless, or sensors.
Drawdown
The peak-to-trough decline in a portfolio or strategy. The risk metric that determines whether you can stay in the trade.
Emissions
New token issuance used to reward participants. Usually dilutive; the yield is a transfer from holders to providers.
FDV
Fully diluted valuation: price × maximum or fully diluted supply. The value of the asset if every token existed today.
Funding rate
The periodic payment between longs and shorts in a perpetual futures market. Positive funding means longs pay shorts.
Impermanent loss
The opportunity cost of providing liquidity versus holding the two assets. It becomes permanent when you withdraw.
L1 / L2
Layer 1 is the base chain; Layer 2 is a scaling system that settles to it. A token can be essential infrastructure and still have weak value capture.
Liquidation
Forced closure of a leveraged position when collateral falls below the maintenance requirement. Cascades can move prices far beyond fundamentals.
LST / LRT
Liquid staking token / liquid restaking token. A transferable claim on staked assets; adds smart-contract and depeg risk.
MEV
Maximal extractable value. Profit from ordering, including, or censoring transactions. Often paid by ordinary users as slippage.
Multisig
A wallet requiring multiple signatures to move funds. Standard for treasuries and protocol admin keys.
MVRV
Market value to realized value. A Bitcoin cycle metric comparing market cap to the aggregate cost basis of holders.
NVT
Network value to transactions. A valuation ratio for monetary assets; noisy but useful as a regime check.
Open interest
The value of outstanding derivative contracts. Rising OI with rising price suggests new leverage; falling OI suggests closing.
Oracle
A service that brings external prices or data on-chain. Oracle design is a security property, not a detail.
Perp DEX
A decentralized perpetual futures venue. Typically uses an order book or a vault-based market maker, with on-chain settlement.
Points
A non-transferable accounting unit used to reward activity, often with an implied future airdrop. Not cash flow.
Realized cap
The aggregate value of all coins at the price they last moved. An approximation of the market’s cost basis.
Restaking
Reusing staked assets to secure additional services (AVSs). Adds yield and layered slashing risk.
RWA
Real-world assets. Tokenized credit, treasuries, real estate, commodities, or funds. The legal claim matters more than the token wrapper.
Slippage
The difference between the expected price and the executed price. A hidden cost that rises with size and thin liquidity.
Stablecoin
A token designed to hold a stable value, usually $1. The peg is a promise backed by reserves, collateral, or a mechanism.
TVL
Total value locked. Assets deposited in a protocol. Not revenue, not deposits, and not directly comparable across protocol types.
Unlock
The release of previously locked tokens into circulation. A supply event that can pressure price if demand does not keep up.
Utilization
The share of supplied assets that are borrowed in a lending market. High utilization can block withdrawals and spike rates.
Validator
An entity that proposes and attests blocks in a proof-of-stake network. Validators earn issuance and fees, and can be slashed.
Wash trading
Trading against yourself or a related party to inflate volume. Common in token listings and leaderboard metrics.
