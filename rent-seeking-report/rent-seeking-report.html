Rent-Seeking: A History, and the Top 50 Ways to Collect Rent Today

From feudal dues to app-store fees — how humans have always gotten paid for controlling access to something scarce, and where the durable rents are now.

October 19, 2026

Every item on the long list below is the same trick wearing different clothes: own (or control) something scarce, then charge others for access to it. Economists call the income this produces economic rent — payment above what it costs to bring the asset into use. A landlord, a patent holder, a taxi-medallion owner, and YouTube are all in the same business.

The technical term rent-seeking has a sharper edge: it means extracting that income through political favor or artificial scarcity rather than by creating value — lobbying for a license only you can get, zoning that blocks competitors, a monopoly charter. This report covers both sides honestly: the history of how rents have been captured, and the legitimate modern ways to build rental income streams today.

Part I — A Short History of Rent-Seeking

  • Ancient world Land rents and tax farming. In Rome, Egypt, and Mesopotamia, elites collected rents on farmland they did not work. Rome outsourced tax collection to private publicani who kept everything above the contracted sum — pure rent on a state-granted privilege.
  • Middle Ages Feudal dues and guild monopolies. Lords charged serfs rents, milling fees (you had to grind grain at the lord’s mill), and tolls. Craft guilds and royal charters granted exclusive rights to trade a good or practice a trade — the original licensed moats.
  • 1500s–1800s Enclosure of the commons. Shared grazing and farmland across England was fenced and titled to private owners, who then charged rents on land people had used freely for centuries. Scarcity, manufactured by law, converted directly into rental income.
  • 1600s–1700s Monopoly charters and patents. The Statute of Monopolies (1624) reined in the Crown’s habit of selling exclusive trading rights, while formalizing time-limited patents — a deliberate, bounded form of rent to reward invention. The East India Company held a royally granted trade monopoly for over two centuries.
  • 1800s Railroad land grants and mineral royalties. The U.S. granted railroads roughly 175 million acres; the railroads became some of America’s largest landlords. The oil age created the mineral-royalty model: own the minerals, lease to a driller, collect a percentage forever.
  • Early 1900s Tolls, turnpikes, and the airwaves. Private toll roads and bridges monetized chokepoints. Then radio arrived: a finite spectrum meant governments issued a limited number of broadcast licenses — a rent on thin air that made license-holders rich.
  • 1930s–1950s Medallions and liquor licenses. New York capped taxi medallions in 1937; by 2013 a single medallion traded near $1 million — pure license rent, later destroyed by rideshare. Post-Prohibition liquor-license caps created the same dynamic state by state.
  • Mid 1900s Zoning and occupational licensing. Village of Euclid v. Ambler (1926) blessed zoning, letting incumbents restrict new supply and inflate the rents on existing property. Licensed occupations in the U.S. grew from a handful to over a thousand — each license a small tollbooth on someone else’s labor.
  • Late 1900s IP thickets and financial rents. Pharma evergreening stretched drug monopolies; cable companies held local franchise monopolies. Finance invented its own rents: credit-card interchange fees, carried interest, payment for order flow — fractions of a percent on enormous flows.
  • 2000s–today Platform rents. App stores take ~30% of digital sales; marketplaces charge sellers for access to buyers they aggregated. Data, attention, and compliance costs became the new scarcities. Meanwhile restrictive zoning turned housing itself into one of the great rent machines of the modern economy — and NIMBY politics into textbook rent-seeking.
The pattern across 2,000 years: rents flow to whoever controls the chokepoint — land, a license, a charter, a platform, a patent. Technology keeps creating new chokepoints, and politics keeps manufacturing artificial ones. The durable fortunes were built on genuine scarcity plus legal protection; the fragile ones on favors that a legislature, a court, or a disruptor could revoke.

Part III — What Separates Durable Rents from Fragile Ones

  1. Genuine scarcity beats manufactured scarcity. Beachfront, minerals, and spectrum are scarce by nature. Medallions and licenses are scarce by statute — and statutes change. (Ask a 2013 medallion buyer.)
  2. Legal protection matters. A deed, a patent, a 30-year lease, a recorded royalty interest — rents survive when the right to collect is enforceable without your presence.
  3. Low maintenance wins over time. The best rents need no labor: land leases, royalties, dividends, digital products. Anything requiring daily effort is a job with an asset attached.
  4. Inflation linkage is the quiet compounder. Rents that reset — annual lease bumps, CPI escalators, dividends that grow — hold purchasing power. Fixed payments melt.
  5. Beware the platform’s cut. Building on someone else’s chokepoint (an app store, a marketplace, a social algorithm) means your rent is their rent-seeking. Own the customer relationship when you can.
  6. Diversify the chokepoints. One rental house is concentrated risk; a portfolio across asset types is a rent portfolio. History’s rentier class never held just one toll bridge.
A final distinction worth keeping: collecting rent on something you own or built — a building, a book, a business — is productive. Rent-seeking in the economist’s sense is extracting payment by rigging the rules: the license nobody else can get, the zoning that blocks competitors, the subsidy for existing. The first builds wealth; the second just moves it. Aim to be a landlord, not a lobbyist.
